# THE ASHEN DOMINION
## Bog & Nia — Mississippi

*Somewhere in the former state of Mississippi*

---

## 1. Recreation

The queue had a system, which surprised no one who had served in the Val infantry. Rank went first, then seniority, then whoever had won the dice game behind the supply truck. Sergeant Gorrak, who had lost the dice game and was taking it badly, stood at the end of the line eating a roasted beetle and glaring at the backs of his comrades.

Inside the tent, on a cot built for someone nearly a foot taller, Nia Okonkwo lay on her side with her hands cuffed in front of her and stared at the canvas ceiling.

*Four hundred and thirteen.*

She had counted the first one an hour ago, when the squad's lieutenant ripped the tail out of her costume, examined it, and announced with a delighted laugh that she was "already fitted." She had counted the second when the lieutenant's palm came down on her right buttock and her whole body answered like a struck bell. The bells did chime. They chimed sweetly, in the pleasant tone the Val adored, and that was almost the worst part.

"*Physiology*," she said to the ceiling. "It's a reflex. A stupid, mechanical, stupid reflex."

The big green soldier currently occupied with her was too busy to ask her to repeat herself. He did not seem to mind that she was cursing him. The Val never minded the cursing. They treated it the way a man treats a dog that barks while it wags its tail.

*Tomato. Scotch bonnet. Thyme.*

The litany still worked, though it had worn thin. *Amara. Chidi. Daniel.* She added a name tonight she had not used in weeks: *Pickle.* She had no idea why. She decided it was a tactical mnemonic for the toolkit.

The toolkit. She had managed one look at it, through the gap between two broad shoulders, before the lieutenant tossed it onto the supply stack with a handful of other junk. A dull gray case, scuffed at the corners, sitting beside a crate of ration bars. *Class 5.* She had carried it through an escape pod crash and four miles of Mississippi pine without letting go. They had pried it out of her hands only after the fourth palm print, and she had not told them what it was. She had not told them after the eleventh, either.

She would tell them nothing. She would not say *Hermes* or *Appalachia* or *collar*. Whatever else was done to her tonight, she would hold that one door shut.

"Hey, Pickle," the soldier said, in thick, amused Val, slapping her thigh. "Look at this. She's *clenching*. She does it when I say 'tail.' Does she know she's doing it?"

"Ask her," said a voice from outside the flap.

"I know exactly what I'm doing," Nia said. The ♥ slipped into the middle of it before she could catch it. "Go ♥ choke on your own ♥ boots, you overgrown ♥ lizard."

The tent roared with laughter. Outside, down the queue, someone cheered.

She shut her eyes. Her face burned, and she let it, because shame was a reasonable thing to feel and she could use it. *Hold the corridor. Nine days. Thirty thousand people.* She had held a bridge against a tank division once. She could hold a cot for one night.

But the thought that surfaced next was not about her children, and she hated that it wasn't. It was about a rattling aircar and a fat green idiot's hands kneading her thighs. It was about the way he had thrown her into the pod and sealed the hatch without looking back, because his species had never invented the farewell.

*He's dead,* she told herself flatly. *The monster ate him. Stop it.*

Outside, the Val contractors huddled by the fuel drums, watching the tent flap with their jaws set and their eyes unable to look away. One of them, a thin man with a cracked radio, muttered something about the Geneva Conventions. The man beside him told him to shut up before somebody heard.

At the far end of the perimeter, the night shift lamps flickered, though nothing had touched them. A pine needle shivered on the hood of the nearest truck, then another. Sergeant Gorrak stopped chewing and looked at the dark treeline, and then, with great reluctance, down at his own boots.

The ground was humming.

In the tent, one of the bells at Nia's chest chimed again, very softly, with no one touching it.

---

## 2. Clobberin' Time

The first thing to die was the fuel drum.

It did not die so much as become a column of orange light, and the shockwave knocked three contractors flat and sent Sergeant Gorrak's roasted beetle into the dark. Then the treeline came apart. A pine the width of a truck went over sideways, and something stepped through the gap where it had stood.

It was a powered suit, but not a Dominion one. It was nine meters of salvaged plating in mismatched colors, with a school-bus door welded over one shoulder and a sticker of a cartoon cat on the chest, still bright under the floodlights. The cockpit glass sat in the middle of the chest like a cyclops's eye, and behind it, in a pair of oversized goggles, was a girl of maybe twenty with her hair in a lopsided ponytail.

She was laughing, and she did not stop even as she raised the right fist.

"It's clobberin' time!"

The fist came down on the Val field kitchen. The field kitchen stopped being a field kitchen.

"HOLY VAL!" Gorrak bellowed from where he was lying. "Contact! *Contact!* Is that the Old Guards?"

Nobody answered him, because the answer arrived in the form of gunfire. Humans came out of the trees on both flanks, a dozen, then twenty. They wore camouflage jackets, hunting vests, and a few pieces of stolen Dominion armor. They carried rifles, shotguns, and one grim-faced grandmother with a belt-fed machine gun, and every one of them wore a clean white armband stitched with a red cross and a number.

Under the tent canvas, Nia heard the shooting begin and went completely still.

*Armbands.* She had not seen armbands since the first month of the war. Somebody out there, in the ruins of the world, was still insisting on being a lawful combatant. She almost laughed. It would have been a terrible sound, and the soldier on top of her would have taken it for the wrong thing entirely.

He did not take it for anything. He had stopped moving at the first explosion and was staring toward the flap with his trousers around his knees and an expression of pure, stupid betrayal.

"Sir," said a voice from outside. "Sir, we're being *attacked.*"

"I can hear that!" the soldier snapped. "I'm on my *turn!*"

"There's a robot, sir. A big one. It has a *cat* on it."

Nia, whose hands were cuffed in front of her and whose entire body was still humming from the last palm print, did the only reasonable thing available. She drew both knees to her chest and drove them into his chest with everything she had left.

It was not enough, with the collar on. It was enough. Two hundred and forty kilos of Val infantryman went through the tent flap backward, taking the flap with him, and landed in the mud in a tangle of canvas and swearing.

"*Pickle,*" she hissed to nobody, and rolled off the cot.

The camp was in pieces. The suit had dropped a truck onto the dice-game crowd, and the Val were rallying behind the wreck, firing heavy rifles that spat green bolts of light. The bolts flashed off the suit's plating and left black scars. The girl in the cockpit shrieked with delight at every one. Down the slope, the armbanded humans fought in disciplined pairs, ten seconds of fire and then a shift, the way the old Accord manuals taught it. A Val sergeant who tried to charge them took six rounds in the knees from six different directions and sat down in surprise.

The human contractors were the ones in real trouble. They had been standing in the open with their rifles pointed in the wrong direction, and now they were realizing which side of the line they were on. The thin man with the cracked radio dropped his weapon and raised both hands high. A boy with an armband, scarcely sixteen, looked at him over the sights of a hunting rifle, then past him, and lowered it.

Nia crawled. The ground shook under her hands, bells chiming with every lurch, a sweet and merry sound in the middle of hell. She did not care. The supply stack was fifteen meters away. The gray case sat on top of a ration crate, exactly where she had last seen it, and nobody was guarding it now, because every Val in the camp was busy dying or yelling.

She was ten meters away when a shadow fell across her.

Gorrak stood over her, breathing hard, with a green-black burn across his scalp and a dull orange gleam in his eyes. He had lost the beetle, the dice game, and his entire evening, and in his enormous hand was the loose end of her leash.

"No, no, no," he said. "You're *mine,* little rabbit. Finders keepers."

Behind him, the nine-meter suit turned its head.

## 3. The Knight of Vesper

Nia did not hit him.

She could have. Point-blank, with the cuffs for a club, she might have rung his skull hard enough to buy three seconds. But three seconds was all it would buy, and she had spent twelve hours learning exactly how little the collar left her. A Val sergeant who got angry would not stay down. He would stay *angry*, and there was a leash in his fist.

So she did the other thing.

She arched her back, put her knees in the mud, and wiggled. The bells sang. The tail, which she had never forgiven, swayed in a lazy arc, and a hundred and ten kilos of soft, flushed, thoroughly abused backside moved from side to side in the floodlight like a pendulum in a hypnotist's hand.

Gorrak's eyes dropped. The rest of his head followed. It was the single most predictable thing she had ever seen a man do.

*Four hundred and fourteen,* she thought bitterly. *You can count this one as reconnaissance.*

"Oh, you *are* a good rabbit," he breathed.

The shadow behind him closed like a lid.

A steel fist the size of a refrigerator came down around his torso and lifted him straight off the ground. Gorrak roared, more in outrage than in fear, and braced both arms against the fingers. The metal screamed. The Val were built for exactly this, shoulders like bridge cables and bones like pig iron, and for a moment the fingers actually began to part.

"Hold him!" a voice yelled from somewhere high above.

"I AM holding him!"

"Hold him *harder!*"

"That's not a thing!"

The suit stopped arguing and slammed him into the ground. It did it again. The third time the earth bounced, and by the fifth Gorrak had gone slack, mouth open, a green hand still curled around the end of Nia's leash. The girl in the cockpit gave a delighted whoop and dropped him on the other side of a wrecked truck as though discarding a candy wrapper.

Nia exhaled and began to crawl again.

She got four meters. That was when she saw the line breaking.

The Old Guards had been winning for ten minutes, and they had been winning against *contractors*. Humans in stolen vests who had joined the Dominion for food had folded the moment the shooting got serious. The Val had not folded. The Val were a species that had conquered a planet, and cornered, they did not retreat. They *swelled*. A pair of infantrymen had thrown aside their rifles and were charging the armband line with bare hands, taking rifle rounds in the chest like thrown pebbles. A grandmother with a machine gun was backing up. A boy with a hunting rifle had stopped firing and was simply staring.

*One suit,* Nia thought. *One suit isn't going to be enough.*

A black shape went over the top of it.

It cleared the suit's shoulder in a single clean vault, cape spread behind it like a pair of wings, and in midair the figure's arms snapped out. Three thin silver shapes spun away from each hand, trailing cable, and struck the charging Val across the shins, the wrists, the throat. The cables wrapped and cinched. Two soldiers hit the dirt in a tangle, rifles ripped from their grips and clattering away across the mud.

The figure landed in a crouch. Black cowl, white bat-edged ears of armor at the temples, a gray streak running through the hair that spilled out of the back of the hood.

Nia's breath stopped.

"*Darkwing,*" someone near her whispered. A wounded armband, flat on his back, was staring at the sky with the face of a child at Christmas. "Oh my God. It's *him.*"

It was. She had seen him once, across a conference table at the Accord's first planning summit, a lean silent figure in the corner who had said nothing for two hours and then solved their logistics problem in a single sentence. The Knight of Vesper. A billionaire in a mask with no powers at all, who had spent twenty years beating monsters and gods with nothing but money, brains, and a great deal of stubborn courage. Both governments had put his name on the same list. Everyone, *everyone*, had assumed he'd died in the first week.

He had to be sixty now. He moved like thirty.

A gloved hand dipped to his belt and came up full of small round canisters. He threw them in a flat arc, almost lazily, and each one burst against a Val face with a soft *pff*. Pale yellow powder bloomed. The nearest soldier sneezed, then sneezed again, then folded in half, hacking, tears streaming from both eyes and the rifle tumbling out of his hand. A second went down beside him. Then a third.

"Clobber 'em!" Darkwing called.

"AYE-AYE, BOSS!"

The suit did not so much advance as arrive. It scooped up a wrecked truck bed in one hand and used it as a broom, and the remaining Val soldiers went over in a green wave.

It was over in under a minute. The camp lay shattered and smoking, the Val soldiers tied in bundles of silver cable, the contractors on their knees with their hands on their heads, and the thin man with the cracked radio weeping with open relief. The girl in the cockpit had popped her hatch and was standing on the shoulder of the suit with both fists in the air, hollering at the stars.

Nia did not get up. She lay in the churned mud with her cuffs under her chin, the tail drooping behind her, and listened to her own bells settle one by one into silence.

A boot came down in the mud beside her face.

She looked up. Past the black boots, past the cape, to the cowl and the gray eyes beneath it. He was not looking at the collar. He was not looking at the costume, or the piercings, or the leash. He was looking at her face, with the specific, unreadable attention of a man who had once recognized a woman across a conference table, and was now deciding what to do about it.

"Black Huntress," said Darkwing quietly.

Nia opened her mouth to say something dry and brave, and what came out, to her eternal horror, was a small, helpless, chiming "♥."

## 4. Orange Clobberess

Rin did not carry her like a prisoner. She carried her like a kitten she had found in a ditch.

The suit's left arm cradled Nia against its chest plating, the fingers curled loosely around her legs, one thumb positioned with great care so that it didn't press against anything with a bell on it. The hatch above her was open. Rin sat with her goggles pushed up into her hair and her boots swinging over the edge, steering with a pair of levers and humming something that sounded like a cartoon theme song.

"I'm Yan-Rin," she said, leaning out to look down. "Rin. My codename's Orange Clobberess, but nobody uses it."

"Why not?" Nia said. It came out hoarse. She had not intended to talk. Eleven hours of talking to Val in the dark had left her with a policy of silence, but the girl was twenty and radiant and had just thrown a truck at a lizard-man, and the policy lasted about four seconds.

Rin dropped her voice an octave, scrunched up her face into a craggy scowl, and growled, "'*Clobberess* isn't even grammatically correct.'" Then she collapsed into giggles. "That's Boss. He says I should've picked something else, but I like it! I'd paint the suit orange too, if I had paint. He says that'd make me stand out like an idiot in the middle of the woods."

"He's right," said Nia.

"I *know* he's right. That's what's so annoying."

Behind them, the Old Guards were working through the wrecked camp with quick, practiced efficiency. Two men were stripping Val rifles into a pile. The grandmother with the machine gun was sitting on an ammo crate eating the lieutenant's rations. Eight bound Val soldiers lay in a row like felled logs, still sneezing, each with a pair of silver cable loops locked around the ankles. Gorrak, the one who'd held her leash, was being dragged by four men on a tarp. He had not woken up.

Nia watched him go with a clinical eye. "What are you planning to do with them?"

"Oh, the usual." Rin said it with the casual brightness of someone discussing the weather. "Some of them'll cut a deal. Others are good for prisoner exchanges. The Dominion's Appalachian garrison is *super* flexible about that kind of thing. They'll trade you three Val for one Old Guard if you're polite about it."

"Trade."

"Yeah! Sometimes they pay in medicine."

Nia said nothing.

She had held a corridor in Baltimore for nine days. She had watched a Val colonel receive a bribe once, in the early occupation, and thought *that's the end of the dream.* Here, six years in, she was being carried through a pine forest by the nicest girl in the resistance, who was explaining the going rate on captive aliens. The Dominion's rank and file were corrupt, and so, apparently, were the people fighting them. Corruption, it seemed, was the one resource that had survived the war with its supply lines intact.

Even so, even so. A tied Val could still be dangerous. She watched one of them struggle against the cable, shoulders flexing, until the metal groaned. The silver loops held, barely. *Troublesome breed,* she thought. *Don't let them rest.*

"They'll be fine for the ride," Rin said, catching her look. "Boss rigs the loops to shock them if they pull too hard. Sneeze powder's just to slow them down."

"Where are we going?"

"The hideout. Don't worry, it's nice! Well. It's a cave. But there's soup."

*Soup.* Nia's stomach, long accustomed to a different diet, made a small and completely traitorous sound.

Ahead of them, through the pines, Darkwing walked alone. He did not look back, and he did not speak, but every few steps he lifted a hand and a gray-gloved finger touched a spot on the side of his cowl. She suspected he was listening to the forest. At the edge of her vision, under his cape, something moved against his belt: a dull gray case, scuffed at the corners.

Her toolkit.

He had picked it up from the supply stack on his way out. She had watched him do it. He had stopped, turned it over once in his hands, and then, instead of tossing it to a scavenger or logging it with the rest, tucked it under his arm as though it were a thing worth keeping.

"Rin," Nia said, very carefully. "Does your boss usually take the junk?"

"Hm? No. Boss never takes anything. He says looting makes us no better than the Val." She squinted ahead. "Huh. That's weird."

Darkwing stopped walking.

He did not turn around. He simply said, in a low, even voice that carried through the pines without effort, "Rin. Put the Huntress down gently. And bring the suit's lights down to nothing."

"Boss?"

"Now."

The pines went black. In the silence that followed, something very faint and very distant began to buzz, a thin electric rattle like a fly in a jar, coming from the south, and growing.

## 5. The Bunker

The wire was exactly where he expected it to be.

Darkwing crouched at the base of a pine and ran two gloved fingers through the needles, the way another man might check a pulse. He stopped when he found it, a hair-thin line of copper buried an inch deep and running off through the roots. He drew it up, clipped it to a device the size of a cigarette case, and flipped a switch without ceremony.

Half a kilometer away, a flare screamed into the sky and burst orange over the canopy. It was a tracer, one clean streak, aimed nowhere near them. The buzzing in the south swung toward it like a compass needle. The beating of wings rose into a frantic whine, then dwindled, then thinned into nothing.

"Scout drone," Darkwing said. "Two minutes of patience, and it would've had our heat signature. Rin, lights."

"Yes, Boss."

The suit's lamps came back up. Nia lay in the suit's palm, looking up at the man in the cowl, and felt something she had not felt in six years.

It wasn't hope. Hope was too big a word. It was recognition. He had planted a wire in a forest, in the dark, on the *chance* a drone might come, and when it came he had been two steps ahead of it without breaking his stride. This was not a man who had survived. This was a man who had *continued*. After the Meridian Line and the collapse and six years of ash, he was still doing precisely what he had always done, which was refusing to be a victim of the situation.

*One of ours,* she thought. *He's one of ours.*

The thought was so warm that she nearly missed the faint, absurd flutter of her own bells as the suit stepped over a root.

---

The hideout was a U.S. Army storage bunker, a concrete throat cut into the side of a hill, with a rust-eaten blast door that Rin opened with a pulley and a lot of enthusiasm. Inside, someone had hung bedsheets from the pipes and strung bulbs from a car battery. There were bunks and cook-fires and a wall map pinned with a hundred different colors of thread. There was soup. Nia could smell it from the door, and her stomach made its small treacherous sound again.

They put her on a cot in a side room, and four women came to look after her.

None of them were professionals. She could tell at a glance: one had been a dental hygienist, one taught second grade, one was a retired postal worker with a rosary around her wrist, and the youngest, barely nineteen, was learning on the job and trying to hide how much she shook. They cut the cuffs off with bolt cutters, cleaned the scrapes on her knees and elbows, and checked her for fractures with great gentleness. When they got to the collar, they looked at it, looked at each other, and left it alone. The nurse who had been a hygienist whispered something about "doctors at the Clinic."

The piercings were a different matter. They tried pliers, a jeweler's saw, and a small, hopeful application of vinegar. The rings did not so much as scratch.

"I'm sorry," the schoolteacher said. "We just can't. Not without cutting you."

"Leave them," Nia said. She sounded calmer than she felt. "They're Val alloy. They'll outlast us all."

The nurses nodded, relieved, and started gathering their instruments. Nia watched them, lying very still, and spoke before she could change her mind.

"One more thing. The beads."

They stopped.

She had hoped one of them would simply understand. Nobody did. The schoolteacher stood with a roll of gauze in her hand, her face doing something complicated. "The..."

"The ones you took out." Nia stared at the ceiling. She had decided, somewhere on the road, that the right tone was *clinical*. "Put them back."

"Back *where?*" the youngest whispered.

"Where they belong."

The silence in the room was the kind that has a weight. The postal worker crossed herself. The schoolteacher opened her mouth, closed it, and finally said, in the brave voice of a woman who had survived six years by not asking questions, "Of course. Whatever you need."

They did it quickly, with eyes averted, and without a word of comment. Nia let them, her jaw set, her gaze fixed on a water stain above the bunk. She offered no explanation. The honest explanation was that the devices processed her waste, that she had not eaten ordinary food in a very long time, and that a body can be trained into a dependency faster than it can be retrained out of one. The dishonest explanation, which was the one she told herself, was *tactical maintenance of cover.*

*Sanitation,* she thought grimly. *It's sanitation.*

They found her a pair of loose overalls, soft with age and two sizes too big, and helped her into them. The cloth was clean and smelled of lye soap. For a few seconds she sat on the edge of the bunk with her face half-turned, one hand flat against the faded denim, before she noticed the youngest nurse watching her with an expression that was equal parts pity and awe.

"You're really her," the girl whispered. "My brother had your poster."

"Tell him I said sorry about the haircut," Nia said.

---

Darkwing came in after they had gone. He did not sit. He stood at the foot of the cot with his cowl pushed back, revealing the weathered face of a man in his sixties with a tight gray beard and eyes that had not stopped moving.

"The rings," Nia said, before he could speak. "They carry tracking beacons. Val alloy. They'll have a location on me within the hour."

"They did," he said. "I've taken care of that."

"How?"

"We reverse-engineered the pulse code three years ago. A few of the captured units we trade away aren't as broken as the Dominion thinks." He gave a small, dry shrug. "Your beacons are still broadcasting. They're broadcasting from a truck headed toward Georgia."

Nia stared at him. Then, for the first time in a very long time, she laughed. It was a short, cracked sound, but it was real.

He let her finish. When she had, he said, without any change of tone, "Vesper is gone. The Manor, the Cave, Alfred. The children I trained. I lost all of it in the first month." He said each item like an entry in a ledger. "I've been rebuilding what I can. Rin is the first one who stayed."

"She's good," Nia said.

"She's *loud,*" said Darkwing, and something moved at the corner of his mouth that might, in a different decade, have become a smile. "She's also very good."

Through the wall came the faint sound of a girl singing a cartoon theme in two different keys. Nia closed her eyes and listened.

*Humanity isn't lying down,* she thought. *It isn't. Not all of it.*

When she opened them, Darkwing had set something on the foot of her cot, and he was looking at her very steadily.

It was a gray case, scuffed at the corners, with a stenciled mark on the lid that read **CLASS 5**.

"I think," said Darkwing quietly, "you'd better tell me where you got this."

## 6. The Escort

Nia told it the way she would have given a field report, in short sentences, with her eyes on the water stain.

"There was a Val. A sergeant, low rank, on provisional reserve status. He bought me. He wanted something in the depot, and it happened to be the same thing I wanted." She touched the lid of the case with one finger. "We struck a deal. He incited the Broker's people into hiring a supervillain to raid an orbital depot. The villain turned on us. The depot came apart. I got into a pod with this, and he was..."

She stopped.

The pause went on longer than she meant it to. The bulbs hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a pot lid clattered, and a woman laughed.

"...he was in the other pod," she finished. "The one that was swallowed. He's likely dead."

Darkwing did not move. He did not ask what *deal* meant, or why a Val sergeant had bothered to bargain, or what the strange, lingering silence had been holding up. He had spent forty years learning which questions to leave on the table. He simply nodded once, as though she had handed him a coordinate, and let the silence close over it.

"I've heard about the depot," he said. "The collapse is all the contractors are talking about. Their alert level is up, and there's a lot of nervous radio traffic. But the Dominion hasn't sent search parties into North America. Not yet. They're either embarrassed or recalculating." He folded his arms. "Either way, we can't stay here. There have been too many skirmishes in this valley. Tonight's patrol was the third in a month. We'll evacuate by dawn."

"You'd abandon the base?"

"I've abandoned bigger." He said it without self-pity. "Tell me about the villain."

"Pale Murderess. Sophia Ivanova. She had full powers, no collar."

That made him go still in a different way. His eyes narrowed.

"She's surfaced, then." He began to pace the short length of the room, three steps and a turn. "Interesting. She's an assassin's assassin, and she does not come cheap. Only a syndicate with serious money could have hired her, and that's not a band of road raiders. The Cinder Kings are too small to afford her. There is somebody behind the Broker, and I think I know who." He stopped. "The League of Shi. They've been buying talent since the occupation began. It's the kind of thing they'd do."

Nia had heard the name once, a rumor in a prison yard. She filed it away beside the other things she would think about later.

"Still," Darkwing continued, "it's a mercy the Dominion's weapons never reached them. If the League had a depot's worth of Class 5 equipment, we'd have a very different war." He turned. "Which brings me to the toolkit. How do you intend to use it?"

"A doctor." She said it carefully. "Dr. Hermes, at the Free Clinic's Appalachian branch. He claims he can unlock collars if he has the right tools."

"I know him." Darkwing's mouth twitched. "He's insufferable, and he's not lying."

"Could you do it?"

"With time." He said it flatly. "Months, perhaps. I don't have months. Hermes does the work in weeks, because he's been waiting for this for years." He picked up the case, weighed it, and set it gently back on the cot. "You'll take it to him. And Rin will go with you."

Nia stared. "Rin?"

"As your escort."

"But isn't she needed here? You'll be evacuating. You'll be exposed. She's your—"

"My sidekick. Yes." He did not blink. "Nia, listen. If we can remove the null-collars from the Bound, we have a bench of heroes that the Dominion believed it had finished. Atlas Reign. Slipstream. Sunspike. Every one of them, with the powers they were born with. We could turn the tide of this war." He paused. "I don't know of a more important mission on this continent. I'm simply making sure that you travel with a stronger protector than an old man who gets winded on stairs."

"You are *not* winded on stairs."

"Fine. I'm a *careful* old man." He picked up his cowl and turned to the door. "She's been wanting an excuse to take the suit on a real trip. It will be good for her. It'll be terrible for the suit."

Nia looked at the case on the cot. The stenciled letters stared back at her. *CLASS 5.* She had carried it through a crash, a camp, and a long night of palm prints, and she had told nobody what it was. She had not expected to *hand it over.*

Behind the bedsheet wall, a muffled thump was followed by a loud, delighted "BOSS! Did I just hear I get to drive to *Tennessee?*"

Darkwing closed his eyes. It was the only sign of fatigue she had seen on him all night.

"Pack light, Rin," he said, without raising his voice.

"I'M ALREADY PACKED!"

"You are *not.*"

"I'M PACKING!"

Nia leaned back against the wall, hands loose in her lap, and for a moment allowed herself something she had not allowed in six years: she let herself imagine the collar coming off. She imagined her own hands, in her own strength, around a Val's neck.

And, a half-second behind it, uninvited, the picture of a fat green idiot asleep on a couch with his boots on.

*He's dead,* she thought. *Stop it.*

Down the corridor, the bells at her ankles gave a single, treacherous chime.
