# The Ashen Dominion: Bog & Nia

## Chapter: The Road to Tennessee

Rin was, Nia decided somewhere around the third hour, the loudest person she had ever met, and she had once worked a rehab gym full of retired drill sergeants.

The cockpit of the powered suit was built for one and a half people if both of them were generous about elbows. Nia sat wedged in the jump seat behind Rin's shoulder in the loose overalls the nurses had found her, knees up, collar cold against her throat, while the nine-meter machine strode down a deer trail with a motion like a drunk giraffe in a bus. Every step jingled. The bells had been muted by nothing but luck and denim, and each footfall made one of them sing, a clear, lovely, humiliating note.

"That's the nice thing about you," Rin said cheerfully, not turning around. "I always know where you are!"

"I'm sitting directly behind you."

"Yeah, but now it's *musical*."

Rin talked the way other people breathed. She talked about the suit's left knee, which had been a bulldozer's. She talked about the school-bus door, which she had welded on because "a shoulder needs a door, Nia, don't ask me why, it just does." She talked about Darkwing, in a gruff impression so exact that Nia flinched the first time. *Rin. Stop narrating. Rin. We are in a forest. Rin. The trees have ears.* Then she giggled and said the real one had never once said the trees had ears, he'd said *Val have ears*, which was a different thing.

And she talked about machines, which was where Nia began listening properly.

"I'm a bit of an expert, you know," Rin said, tapping a dial with a knuckle. "Even the boss asks me. *Rin, will it blow.* *Rin, can you make it not blow.* I'm the one who worked out how to block the signals from those ring things. Val ones, yours, everybody's. Reverse the pulse code, bounce it back out of the truck. Easy." She wriggled. "I bet I can take a look at your collar *and* the toolkit. I mean, probably *both*. And Dr. Hermes! I can't wait to meet Dr. Hermes! Is it true he's a mutant? Does he have, like, a *lot* of arms?"

Nia looked at the back of her head, at the lopsided ponytail and the goggles pushed up like a second pair of eyes, and could not decide how much of it to believe. A girl who fixed machines with her bare enthusiasm and a salvaged bulldozer knee. A girl who, when Darkwing said *very good, Rin*, had visibly glowed for twenty minutes.

But Nia had watched the suit slam a Val sergeant into the dirt until he stopped moving, and she had watched a girl laugh in the middle of it. In six years Nia had not met many people who laughed in the middle of it.

*Keep your hopes up,* she told herself. *Not high. Up. Like a flag. A small, sensible flag.*

*He's dead,* said another part of her, without being asked. *Stop picturing the couch.*

"You're quiet," Rin said.

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Trees."

Rin accepted this at once, which was one of her better qualities.

---

The trouble began where the land flattened into old state line: a drowned highway, a sign with the name of one state on its front and the other on its back, both rusted past reading. The air was heavy. Nia noticed it first as a sort of pressure, the way a room feels when someone has stopped speaking mid-sentence. She felt it in the dense skin of her forearms, in the faded, collared place where her old connection to the living world used to hum like a refrigerator in the next apartment. It was almost nothing now. It was still enough to tell her that the woods around them were *wrong*. Not silent. Hushed.

"Rin. Stop the suit."

"Why? Is it the bells? I can find you a sock."

"Stop."

The suit stopped. A deer stepped onto the road.

It was a handsome doe, and she was haloed in violet, a thin crawling light like oil on water that clung to her fur and trailed from her eyes. She walked with the loose-jointed care of a puppet. Behind her came a second, then a coyote, then a pack of coyotes, then a wild boar the size of a motorcycle, and the light on all of them was the same sick purple.

"Oh," Rin said, in a very small voice, for the first time in three hours. And then, in her full voice: "Oh, *cool*."

"Rin, it is not cool."

"The *boss* said something about this!" She was already hauling the levers. "He said, *animals, purple, don't let them bite you, don't tell Rin it's cool*, so he totally predicted it. Hold on to something!"

Nia held on to the strap, her own knee, and Rin's seat, in that order.

The suit moved like a cartoon of itself. It caught the boar by the tusks and flung it, spinning, into the treeline with a sound like a dropped piano. It stamped, and the coyotes scattered, then reformed, then scattered again as a car door, ripped off a dead truck, spun through them edge-first. "It's clobberin' time!" Rin shrieked, joyfully, a half-second late, and Nia thought: *That is the worst catchphrase I have ever heard. Please never change.*

The doe lunged for the glass. The suit's hand caught her out of the air as gently as a child catching a firefly, and set her down in the weeds on her side. She did not get up.

"Sorry, Bambi!" Rin called.

It took under two minutes. When it was done the road was carpeted in bodies, a boar, a dozen coyotes, a doe, a pair of foxes, a fat opossum, and the suit stood steaming in the middle of it, one shoulder dented, the cat sticker on its chest miraculously unscratched.

"See?" Rin turned in her seat, grinning, her goggles knocked sideways. "Easy. We should keep moving before the smell gets in."

Nia didn't answer. She was looking out through the glass.

The purple light had not gone out.

It had lifted off the bodies. It rose in thin, slow threads, one off each animal, one off every dead thing, and it did not drift upward and fade, as any ordinary glow would. It hung at head height, trembling, in the still air. Then, thread by thread, with no wind to push it, it began to turn. Toward the suit. Toward the glass.

"Rin," Nia said, quietly, "how well sealed is this cockpit?"

Rin looked at the seam where the old bus-door weld met the chest plate. A hairline crack there had gone faintly violet.

"...Define 'well,'" Rin said.

---

The purple threads hit the suit and went in through the seam like smoke through a keyhole. Nothing happened. Rin patted the dashboard, said "Good girl," and shoved both levers forward anyway. The nine-meter machine broke into a lumbering run down the drowned highway, flinging a dead coyote off its boot.

That was when the trees began to move.

Nia felt it before she saw it, a long groaning shudder rolling up out of the ground and through the frame of the suit, into the seat, into her spine. Then the glass filled with it. Oaks, pines, a whole sagging stand of sweetgum on the left bank: they tore their roots out of the earth with a sound like wet cloth ripping, dirt raining off them in clods, and they began to walk. Not run. Walk. Branches swayed as if feeling for the air. The hollows in their trunks had gone dark and deep, and every one of them had a face in it, mouths stretched in a long soundless wail, eyes pulled wide, the faces of people who had been told for six years that dying was the end of the trouble and had been lied to.

"Oh no," Rin whispered. "Oh no, they're *sad*."

"Rin. Eyes forward."

"I can't hit sad things!"

"They are hitting *you*!"

A leaf-covered fist the size of a refrigerator came swinging out of the dusk. Rin yelped, caught the branch in the suit's hand, and, since there was nowhere obvious to put a tree, hurled it back to the exact spot on the bank where it had been standing. It stood there a moment, blinking its wooden eyelids. Then it began to walk again.

"That's not fair," Rin said.

And Nia, wedged in the jump seat with her knees against her chest, shuddered.

It was not fear. She would have known fear; fear was an old tenant. This was a deep, rolling hum that came up through the floor in step with the stamping of roots, and it had found every ring the Val had ever put on her. The four on each side, the one at the center, the pair on her chest, all of them taking up the vibration like tuning forks and answering it in their own clear voices. The bells did not ring so much as *sing*. The collar buzzed against her pulse. Her breath went short and shallow and strange.

*No,* thought Nia Okonkwo, who had held the Baltimore corridor nine days. *No. Not here. Not in front of a child.*

She was twenty. Rin was twenty. Nia had decided this meant child.

And it was all, every bit of it, that overgrown cactus's fault. That *pickle*. Leaving her in a sealed pod with a toolkit and a launch button, and no goodbye, and calling it self-sacrifice, as if he knew what the word meant, as if he were the kind of idiot who could die without clearing it with her first, and now she was a woman on the edge of a battle with her thighs clenched around her own pulse, and the worst part was that the hum was in the shape of his voice if she let it be. She did not let it be. She absolutely did not.

"Ah...♥ Um...♥ ..." She cleared her throat hard enough to hurt. "T-There's a supervillain behind this. Isn't there? Something like this doesn't just *happen*. The real mastermind must be hiding nearby. Somebody *steering*."

"You okay back there?" Rin said, ducking a swing. "You're doing the squeaky thing."

"It is the seat. It is a squeaky seat."

"It sounds *really* squeaky."

"Reconnaissance, Rin."

"Oh!" Rin said, with enormous respect. "Cool."

The suit surged. A pine came down on its shoulder and Rin shouldered it off, the bus door flapping, and then a second, a third, a fourth, and Nia stopped counting trees and began counting the *line* of them, because there was a line. She saw it as the suit turned, a long uneven wall of moving trunks, shoulder to shoulder on both banks, closing behind them with the unhurried patience of a net. Not a mob. A herd. Being driven, or being gathered.

She pressed her palm to the glass and, out of nothing but old habit, reached.

It used to be that she could say *friend* to a tree and the tree would hear. Under the collar there was almost nothing, a dim pilot light, and yet when she reached she felt a thing she had not expected: no malice. No command. Only pain, and a terrible, enormous *crowding*, as if every root in the forest had been shoved into too small a room.

"They're not doing this," she said slowly. "Something's using them. A lot of somethings."

"Well, *tell* them to stop!"

"I'm collared, Rin!"

"Then tell them *loudly*!"

The highway ahead lurched. A pine the width of a water tower swung across it and stayed there, a gate. Behind it another. Behind that, rank on rank of trunks, branches linked like arms, the faces in their hollows lifted toward the cockpit glass with a patience that made Nia's skin go cold.

Every mouth opened at once.

The voice that came out was one voice and thousands, a wind through a drainpipe, a rasp of bark on bark. Nia knew it. She had never heard it before in her life and she knew it the way you know a bad dream by the second note.

"*Not... buried...*"

"Boss said not to answer them," Rin whispered.

"Boss said *a lot* of things," Nia whispered back, and one of the bells chimed, small and bright, in the quiet.

---

Rin did what she always did when a situation exceeded her patience, which was to make the suit do something showy.

The head section spun on its neck ring with a grinding whirr, and from the dome slid a bristle of instruments: two telescopes, a fan of antennae, a lens that looked suspiciously like the bottom of a bottle, and a small brass horn that Nia would have sworn had been a gramophone's. They swept the dark in a slow arc. The gate of pines in front of them creaked closer. Somewhere behind, the line of walking trunks drew its net a little tighter.

"Okay, okay, okay," Rin murmured, one eye squashed against a viewfinder. "Trees, trees, trees, *more* trees, a very sad squirrel, trees..." She stopped. "Oh. *Oh.* There's a pillar."

"A what?"

"A pillar of purple! On the mountain!" She flung her free hand at the glass. "Up top, on the peak, a big straight beam going right up into the clouds, and everything's kind of... *leaning* toward it. Like flowers. Creepy flowers." She squinted. "Is that where your supervillain's hiding? I guess they really like to stand out. Say what you want about the boss, but he *always* says never stand out."

A pine took a swing at the suit's knee. Rin, without looking, flicked a missile out of a shoulder tube and turned it into mulch.

"Sorry!" she said to the tree. "Sorry, that was rude!"

Nia leaned in beside her and looked.

She could see it now through the gap in the canopy: a faint violet column rising from a dark hump of ridge, thin as a thread from here, standing so still it seemed painted on the sky. The trunks all around them swayed toward it, ever so slightly, the way a congregation sways toward a hymn. The pressure in her chest had the same direction. Whatever was wrong in these woods, it ran uphill, and it came from there.

So there was a choice, and it was an ugly one.

West, past the drowned sign and the walking forest, lay Alabama, and the road to a doctor Nia had been told could open her collar. Dr. Hermes, who might have many arms. A door out of six years of this. She wanted it with a fierceness that embarrassed her. It would be reasonable to go around. It would be *sensible*. A woman with a toolkit in her lap and a child at the controls had no business marching up a mountain to meet whatever stood at the center of a haunted forest.

And yet.

*Nia. Always thinks about civilians,* said a lazy voice in her head, one that smelled faintly of cockroach skewers. She told it to be quiet. But the memory refused to go away: the pressure of the trees' pain, thousands of lives squeezed into one dark room. Somewhere beyond these woods were farms and ration districts and settlements without a single hero between them and this. Whatever lit that pillar was not going to stop at a state line.

She looked at Rin. Rin looked at her.

Rin's face, for once, held no giggle. It held a question, and under it a kind of earnest and desperate readiness, the look of a kid waiting for the grownup to say it was okay. *The boss would know,* it said. *I don't. Tell me.*

Nia took a breath.

"Clobber 'em," she said.

Rin's whole face lit up like a switchboard.

"*Yes, ma'am!*"

She slammed a lever the color of a traffic cone. Somewhere under Nia's feet, a bank of pipes that had once been a school bus's exhaust coughed twice, spat a gout of orange flame, and lit. The suit crouched. The pines scattered. And then the whole ugly nine-meter machine leapt, rockets screaming, off the broken highway and up into the dark in a long shuddering arc, the bells on Nia's body ringing like a church on fire.

The forest dropped away. Treetops rushed past, a sea of reaching, wailing faces. Wind roared at the glass. The ridge swung up ahead, the violet column standing in front of it like a spear.

Nia gripped the strap with both hands and said, through her teeth:

"Hey. Couldn't we have reached Alabama a lot sooner if we'd used this earlier?"

"It can't stay in the air very long, you know," Rin said, very brightly.

"How long is 'very long'?"

Rin did not answer. She was watching a small needle on the dash, a needle that had been resting comfortably in the green and was now sliding, with the calm of a thing that did not care what you thought, toward a thin red line.

"About," she said, "this much."

Below them the mountain came on, black and enormous, and the purple light at its crown began, slowly, to pulse.

---

The nine-meter suit hit the mountainside the way a dropped anvil hits a porch.

Rin had meant it to be a landing. It was, technically, a landing, in the sense that the machine's feet were afterwards on rock. The shock traveled up the legs, through the frame, into the jump seat, and from the jump seat into Nia, who discovered that a body fitted with eleven small rings, a bead string and a dozen sensible reasons not to be vibrated could be vibrated all the same. Every bell went off in a single bright clash. Her toes curled in her borrowed boots. Her spine arched. She put the back of her wrist against her mouth, and she said absolutely nothing, and she would be saying nothing about it for the rest of her natural life.

"Whoa, big bounce," said Rin, shaking her head clear. "You good?"

"Fine." A pause. "Seat."

"Right. Squeaky."

Then the purple came up through the stone.

It erupted from the cliff face in a blinding sheet, a flood of violet that turned the cockpit glass to a pane of bruised light, and out of the flood a shape stood up. A giant, human in outline, shoulders like a bridge, built from rock: boulders for fists, a slab of granite for a chest, a head cobbled out of scree. And every stone in it, each individual stone, carried a face. They pressed through the surface like drowning men pressing against ice. Mouths opened and closed. Eyes rolled. A thousand tiny agonies, packed together and set in motion.

It did not seem happy to have visitors. It swung.

Rin caught the fist, barely, with a shriek of metal, and the whole suit skidded a meter back along the ledge. "Spot on!" she crowed, grinning through the strain. "Just like you said, Nia! Boss told me you're a seasoned hero, and he was *right*, you called it straight away!"

"Ah... yeah... I suppose so...♥ " The giant's second fist came in. The ledge shuddered. The bells, with nobody asking them, sang. "L-Let's try to keep the fighting quiet, shall we?♥"

"Quiet! Right! I'll be quiet!" said Rin, who was not.

She was strong. Nia had to give her that. The suit threw the stone giant against the cliff with a crash that shook pebbles loose for a hundred meters, drove a shoulder into its chest and cracked it down the middle. But the crack bled purple light and *closed*. The thing sat up. It was patient. It did not tire.

And then there was a second one.

It climbed out of the slope at the suit's left, a brother of the first, broader, lower, with a face on every flat surface of it screaming something that no longer had a throat. Then a third, shouldering up out of the scree on the right. Rin turned the suit, braced, and took a blow to the back that snapped one of the welded shoulder plates and sent the school-bus door spinning off the mountain into the dark.

"Hey!" she yelled after it. "That was *structural*!"

The giants closed in. The suit, an inch at a time, gave ground. Rin's grin had thinned. A line of sweat ran down her temple into her goggles. "Damn it. Okay. I guess I really don't have a choice. I'll have to use my secret weapon."

A fourth one stood up.

Nia bit her lip hard enough to taste copper.

She had been looking at the wrong thing. For three hours she had watched a girl fling trees and had thought, *strong, fast, fearless*, and had somehow filed the word *seasoned* in the drawer beside the others. But she had not been watching how Rin fought, which was well and loud and *forward*, as if each fight were the last page of a comic. There was no plan in it. There was no *fallback*. It had been enough, so far, because nothing so far had been enough to hurt her. Nia knew the look of a fighter about to find out the difference. She had worn it once, on a bridge outside Baltimore.

"Rin," she said. "We need to retreat."

"Retreat? To *where*?"

"Anywhere. Up. Back. The jets, Rin, now, before—"

The ground split.

It began as a dry crack at the suit's feet and ran, with a snapping sound like a whip, across the ledge, up the slope, and out into the dark, and then it *opened*, wide and slow and wet, a long jagged mouth with rows of stone teeth along both lips. Behind it the whole face of the mountain moved. A brow of rock ground down. Two hollows of cold violet light, each the size of a house, rolled open and found the cockpit.

The mountain had a face. It looked at them with the vast, weary hatred of something that had been disturbed for the last time.

"You've *got* to be kidding me," Rin whispered.

She stood there. For one second, one terrible, ordinary second, a twenty-year-old girl in an overbuilt toy stood frozen at the edge of a grave.

The jaws came down.

Stone teeth closed over the suit's shoulders with a roar of crushing steel. The glass went white with cracks. Nia threw herself across Rin, a hand clamped over the child's goggles, thinking, absurdly, *Amara, Chidi*, and then, *pickle*, and then nothing at all.

There was a flash, white and thin and exact, like a needle through cloth.

The teeth came apart. They did not break so much as *scatter*, in a bright spray of cooked gravel, and the jaw above them lurched up and away with a groan. Cold air rushed in through the shattered glass. Rin was shouting. Somewhere, very high, a stone giant was howling in a voice made of voices.

And from the dark below the ledge, over the ringing in her ears, Nia heard a voice she knew, easy, bored, faintly greasy, the voice of someone strolling across a battlefield as if it were a shopping arcade.

"Pardon me," it said. "If you came to Florence, you could get much better dental work done."

Nia did not move.

In the quiet that followed, one of her bells rang. It was a tiny, bright, clear note, and it did not stop for a very long time.

---

---

"Ugh! A Val soldier!"

Rin said it the way other people say *a cockroach*, and all her good manners went with it. The suit wrenched itself out of the cracked jaw, pried the school-bus-less shoulder free of a tooth that was still trying to bite, and Rin, with an inelegant wriggle, shoved the hatch open and scrambled out through the fissure in a clatter of goggles and elbows. She landed in a crouch on the gravel, a wrench in each fist.

Nia stayed where she was.

She could see it all through the broken glass. A green giant stood in the open below the ledge, eight feet of him, shoulders like a doorway, in a soot-streaked sergeant's coat that had seen better decades. The overhead light was a ghastly violet and made his skin look like pond water. He held a fat, ugly, ill-made Val laser in both hands, and the barrel was glowing the color of a stove coil.

A stone giant, huge and furious, lumbered at him out of the dark.

He lifted the laser, aimed with all the care of a man swatting a fly, and pulled the trigger.

The giant came apart in a pillar of white. A thousand little faces, a thousand tiny shrieks, flew outward and went to gravel. A rain of cooked pebbles pattered over the ledge. The Val soldier looked at the smoking weapon, said "Oof, hot, hot, *hot*," shook his hand, and, with an air of staggering, casual indifference, threw the thing over his shoulder into a ravine.

"That's enough of *that*," he muttered.

It rang off the rocks for quite a long time. Nia listened to it go.

How *ridiculous*, she thought. How absurd. She was thirty-nine years old, she had held the Baltimore corridor nine days, she had a litany for Amara and Chidi and Daniel, and she was sitting in a wrecked toy with her chin trembling and her eyes stinging because of a *Val*. A man who had bought her at a slave market. A man who had put her in a pod and *launched* it. A man who, according to the most reasonable half of her mind, had been dead for four days and eaten by a tentacle monster.

The other half of her mind had stopped speaking altogether. It was busy being a very small, very loud kettle.

"Nia?"

Rin had climbed back up to the shattered glass. Her face, smeared with dust, hung inches from Nia's, wide-eyed and worried. "Nia, are you okay? You look weird. You went all quiet. Did the teeth get you? I can fix teeth, probably. Mostly I fix *knees*, but..."

"Yeah," said Nia. Her eyes had narrowed. "I'm fine."

"You sure? Because the Val is, like, *right there*, and I really think I should clobber him, I'm just waiting for you to say..."

"Wait."

"Wait?"

"*Wait,*" Nia said again, quietly, in a voice with grass in it.

Because the Val soldier, having disposed of his weapon, had not come toward them. He had turned the other way.

Behind a nearby boulder, in the lee of a long slab of fallen cliff, someone was crouched with her head down and her arms over it. A woman. Plump and soft-shouldered, with dark hair coming loose out of a braid, and wearing next to nothing. A black-and-crimson something, a short and *very* generous costume, cut high at the hips and low across the chest, that did not look like it had been sewn so much as *happened* to her. She looked as though she had been caught in the rain, and the rain had been fire.

The Val strolled over, bent down, and lifted her bodily into the air.

Something went quietly white inside Nia.

He settled the woman onto his shoulders like a sack of rice, one big green hand curled around her plump thigh to hold her steady, and, without the faintest sign of hurry, began to jog toward the suit. The woman clung to his head with both hands. Her braid bounced. The rocks shuddered behind them as two more stone giants, one after the other, stood up out of the scree.

"Who," said Nia, "is that *woman*?"

"Dunno," said Rin. "Why? Is she a hero?"

"I have no idea. I don't *care*. It's just..."

It was just that the Val soldier, her Val soldier, her overgrown cactus, her *pickle*, did not have a free hand to hold a woman's thigh with. He had no business having *any* thighs on his shoulders. He had business being dead, and being mourned, and being *sorry*.

"It's just a strange thing," Nia finished carefully, "to carry. That's all."

"Right," said Rin, with total faith.

The giants closed in behind the running Val. The first raised a fist the size of a car. The soldier did not turn his head. He simply said, to the woman on his shoulders, in his flat, greasy, unbothered voice, "Boss, when you're ready," and the woman, who had been sagging and shivering over his skull, lifted her head.

Her face was not the face of a frightened nurse.

She raised both hands.

Nia saw her skin change. It began at the fingertips and rolled inward: a deep blush, a flush of copper, then red, then a red so deep and clean it looked like the heart of a forge. It poured up her arms, across her shoulders, down the front of that outrageous costume and into the very hair on her head, and a light came out of her with it. Not violet. Crimson, rising in a long ringing sheet that swelled and spread and struck the stone giants full in their chests.

The purple screamed.

It did not fade. It *recoiled*, curling away from the red like paper from a flame, and the walking mountains stopped in their tracks with their stony hands raised and a thousand tiny faces wrenched, for a single astonished second, toward the light.

The bells on Nia's body, every last one, went absolutely silent.

---

The woman in red sat on the Val's shoulders for exactly as long as it took the crimson light to settle. Then she leaned down, said something short and sharp into his ear, and, with the neat precision of a diner flicking a crumb off a tablecloth, planted one bare foot against the side of his head and *kicked*.

The Val sergeant left the ground.

He went up in a long green arc, coat flapping, boots kicking at nothing, and as he reached the top of it he said, in a tone of immense and sincere surprise, "*Ouch!*"

He came down in the gravel thirty feet away, rolled twice, and lay still with his arms flung out.

A small, warm, deeply private satisfaction spread through Nia's chest, like hot soup on a cold night. She folded her arms across the front of her borrowed overalls.

*Serves you right,* she thought. *Serves you* so *right.*

"Whoa," Rin breathed. "She *kicks* him. They're not friends?"

"Shh."

The woman hung in the air. Nia had not noticed her rise. She floated above the ledge with her hair loose and lifting around her face, crimson light pouring off her in sheets, and when she spoke it was in a voice with two layers in it, one a small, trembling, apologetic voice, the other deep as a temple bell, and both of them at once.

"I shall bury," she cried, "the exposed singularity, *here*, into this space!"

It was, Nia thought, a rather complicated thing to announce. It sounded like the title of a legal brief. But the light behind the words was real. The red surged upward in a vast pulsing wave and drove at the purple like a broom at a corner, shoving the violet column back, back, up the cliffs, up the long dark shoulder of the ridge, until it was cornered against the summit like a cat in a sack, and the stone giants, one by one, crumbled into harmless heaps with a sigh of rattling gravel.

The pressure in the air eased. The trees below, still on their feet, swayed, and sank their toes back into the soil one by one.

Someone coughed.

Bog had sat up. He brushed gravel off his coat and rolled his stiff shoulder, then stood, not in any hurry, and ambled across the slope until he came to the spot directly beneath the floating woman. He stopped there. He folded his hands behind his back. He tipped his head all the way up, and he gazed, with a face of rapt and shameless admiration, at the large, round, scarlet backside above him, which glowed as though it were itself a small sunrise.

Nia's eye twitched.

"Looking good, Boss," he said, to nobody.

Then he turned away, wiped his palms on his coat, and strolled over to the powered suit as if he had never once been kicked in his life.

"Hey there!" He raised a big green hand and waved up at the cracked glass. "That was a close one, wasn't it? You a hero? That's a *cool* suit you've got. Is that a cat?"

There was a sticker on the chest. It had survived the teeth. It looked a little proud.

"He he he," said Rin, grinning down at him. Her suit had gone slack and companionable. She propped her elbow on the cockpit rim. "Hey, he's pretty reasonable for a Val, isn't he? He killed those rocks and everything!"

She turned to Nia for confirmation and caught the look on her face.

It was not a nice look. It had been building for four days and six hundred miles and it had arrived complete, like a freight train.

"...So," Rin said, more carefully. "What's the plan? Should we crush him?"

"Yes," said Nia. It came out at once, crisp and clear, like a bell.

Then a low sound escaped her, a soft, small, hurt, helpless thing between a laugh and a sob, and she covered her mouth with her hand and breathed through her nose until it stopped.

"Actually," she said, in a voice that did not wobble at all, "I'll talk to him."

"Talk?"

"Set me down, Rin."

Rin looked at her. Then at the Val, who was peering with great interest at the suit's knee. Then back at Nia. Rin, whatever else she was, was not stupid.

"Okay," she said slowly. She reached down and, with surprising gentleness, lifted Nia in one scoop of the great steel palm, out through the shattered glass, past the jagged teeth of the broken pane, and lowered her toward the gravel, toward the green giant who stood there humming a Val drinking song, whistling, scratching one ear, with a smear of purple dust across his coat.

Her boots touched the earth.

All of her bells, together, began to ring.

---

Bog turned at the sound.

He saw the bells first, which was only natural, since they were still chiming. Then the overalls. Then the scar from the left temple to the cheek, and the close-cropped hair full of mountain dust, and the dark eyes that had once stared down a tank division and were presently staring down *him*.

His own eyes went wide. His jaw dropped, and for the space of one breath the great green face was blank as a plate.

Then it broke into a grin so broad, so unguarded, so entirely without shame that Nia, who had spent four days preparing an opening statement, forgot every word of it.

"Hey there!"

He actually waved.

"Looks like your escape pod came down pretty close to the Mississippi side, huh? Good aim. Did you hitchhike the rest? Quite a journey, I bet!" He scratched the back of his neck, sheepish, bashful, the very image of a man discussing a missed bus. "Me, I was in a bit of a rush. Jetpack, you know, no safety features, and I ended up a little too far over on the Alabama side. Total waste of a fuel cell. Then I had to walk. You wouldn't believe what the bears are like around here."

Nobody said anything.

A stone pebble rolled off the ledge and tinkled down the cliff for a long, long time.

Behind Nia, the suit's frame creaked as Rin leaned forward, very slowly, a girl who had watched an entire sequence of plot unfold in front of her and was now trying very hard not to speak.

"*Hitchhike,*" said Nia.

It came out low. It came out like a pot lid lifting.

Four days. She had spent four days in a bunny costume, a collar and a string of Val beads, being *counted*. She had been caught, and bought again, and passed from hand to hand, and sat in a tent with the sound of a queue outside, saying her litany over and over in the dark: *tomato, Scotch bonnet, thyme. Amara. Chidi. Daniel.* And somewhere around the middle of the litany, in the place where a good Christian woman would have put an Amen, she had been putting *him*. A small, absurd, shameful word. And she had been mourning it. She had mourned it *properly*, with the lights off.

And here it stood, scratching its ear.

"A *journey,*" said Nia, "is a thing you take on purpose."

"Right!" Bog nodded. "That's what I said."

"You threw me in a pod."

"With the toolkit." He looked faintly wounded. "You got the toolkit, right? I put a lot of thought into the toolkit."

One of her hands was clenched at her side. She felt it, the muscles of the forearm drawing taut, the fist closing slowly on itself. Under the collar there was almost nothing, a pilot light, a pinch of the strength she used to have. It would be, she knew, as satisfying as hitting a mattress. It would hurt her more than it hurt him. She did it all the same. She took one slow step across the gravel and then another, and the bells rang with each one, high and sweet and shamelessly delighted.

Bog's eyes followed the sound. The grin on his face did not slip, but it did go a little thoughtful, as if some far-off cog in his skull had started to turn.

"Oh," he said, quietly. "You do look different."

He did not mean the overalls.

She stopped in front of him. She had to tip her chin up to look at his face, as she always had. His shadow lay across her boots.

"Bog," said Nia Okonkwo, "I am going to say one thing to you, and then I am going to decide what to do with my hands."

"Sure."

She drew a breath.

Above them, in the red air, a woman in a crimson costume was watching the two of them with a face that was, for the moment, nobody's at all.

---

"Green one."

The voice came down out of the red air like a bell rung in an empty church, and Nia's breath died before it reached the first word.

The woman in crimson had drifted lower. Her hair streamed upward in a wind that was not there. A wide scarlet sash unwound behind her in a long slow ribbon, and her eyes, which had been dark and anxious a few minutes before, were two coals burning with a steady, bottomless red. They were not looking at Nia. They were looking at Bog, with the mild regard of a high court looking at a stain on its floor.

"Do not go speaking to others on your own accord," said the Red Mediatoress.

"Oh, I wasn't," said Bog. "She's an acquaintance."

"*Green one.*"

"Yes, Boss. Sorry, Boss."

She came down the rest of the way. She did not land so much as *settle*, tipping gently out of the air into the arms he had hurriedly spread, and she folded into them in a perfect bridal carry, one arm trailing, one hand curling against the front of his coat. The bells on Nia's body, which had been going off like a fire alarm, fell abruptly quiet.

"The burying is not yet complete," the Mediatoress said. Her words came slow now, each one a little further from the last. "Priya is exhausted. She will need rest. You will protect her. You will remain on high alert against the backlash, the lingering thoughts that the Singularity has stirred. They will want a body. The risk of possession is... not slight." Her eyelids drooped. "Be especially wary..."

The crimson went out of her eyes the way a lamp goes out when the oil runs dry. The glow faded off her skin in a slow ebb, from the shoulders down, until what lay in Bog's arms was a plump, dark-haired woman in a very small costume, snoring, quite gently, against his collarbone.

"...of that," she added, in a small, sleepy, entirely different voice, and slept.

Silence.

Bog looked down at the woman in his arms with an expression of mild bafflement. Then up at Nia.

Nia's eyes narrowed.

They narrowed slowly, by degrees, like the shutters of a shop at closing time, and in the narrow dark between her lashes she looked at the bridal carry. At the tiny costume. At one soft arm hanging down against his coat. At the way his big hand, which had been on *her* thigh, a thigh she had specifically been told was not available, cradled this stranger's head so carefully that it seemed to be afraid of breaking an egg.

"Hey," said Rin, from the suit, in a loud and carrying stage whisper. "Nia. Nia. Who *is* she?"

"I don't know," said Nia.

"Because she just turned *red.*"

"I noticed."

"And she kicked him! But she's also *asleep* on him! Is she his girlfriend? Is she his boss? Is she his *wife?*"

"She is," said Bog, with the cheerful vagueness of a man who had been asked about his tax return, "a lot of paperwork."

The temperature of the ledge dropped about five degrees.

"Paperwork," Nia repeated.

"A *lot*," Bog agreed, with feeling. "You wouldn't believe the forms."

She opened her mouth. She had been about to say one thing to him, and then decide what to do with her hands, and the one thing was still there, hard and bright as a coin on her tongue, and it was a fine thing, a *bruising* thing, and she was just about to...

A bell rang.

Not hers.

It was a low, flat, ugly sound, high on the ridge behind the summit, like a gong struck underwater, and it rolled down the mountain slowly and shuddered through the stones under their feet. Far above them, in the dark corner of the sky where the red light had pinned it, the purple glow flickered, pulsed, and brightened again.

"Oh," said Bog, glancing up. "That doesn't sound like *buried*."

---

"Explain," said Nia.

It was not a request. It came out the way she used to say *drop and give me ten* to a patient who had been skipping his exercises, level, patient, and with enormous quiet force behind it. The gong on the ridge boomed again. Gravel shivered. Overhead, the pinned purple glow rose and fell like a bad pulse.

Bog looked at her. He looked at the sleeping woman in his arms. He looked at Rin, who was hanging over the rim of the cockpit with her mouth open and her eyes enormous, and then up at the mountain, and then at Nia again. He seemed to consider several long and winding answers, and to discard them one by one, like a man going through his pockets for a lost key.

"Okay," he said. "Short version."

"I'm listening."

"The world went haywire because you heroes kept spamming your powers during the war against the Dominion." He said it without any heat, just the way he would have noted that it rained on Tuesdays. "All that astra-siddhi business, over and over, and it kept poking holes in the place. Little holes, big holes, a leaky roof. Then to top it all off, the Murderess went and opened a whole bunch of portals for the tentacle monster, and *that* broke it for good. Kind of like kicking a cracked plate." He shifted the woman a little higher against his chest, and one big green hand slid to rest on her thigh, and began, absently, to stroke it. "This big-bottomed lady here is the one who fixes things."

There was a pause.

It was a long pause. It had a great many things in it.

Rin raised her hand like a student. "I have follow-up questions," she said.

"Later," said Nia.

She did not look away from him. She was running it through, slowly, the way she had once run a rehab patient through a gait cycle, one piece at a time, to find where the weight was wrong. *Heroes spamming powers.* That would be Sunspike and her beams. Thunderhead and his storms. It would be, God help her, herself, awakening the forces of nature and enlisting them as allies in every ditch from Baltimore to the Appalachians. Every one of them a hole in a roof. And no one had known. No one had even *thought*.

*And the Murderess,* she thought. *The Murderess is dead.*

But she was not dead. Nia knew it the moment the thought touched the word. If the Murderess had been dead, Bog would not have said *the tentacle monster* so smoothly, so easily, with the faint sliding of the eyes of a man who has stepped over a particular crack in the floor on purpose.

"And where," Nia said, evenly, "is Sophia Ivanova now?"

Something crossed his face. It was very quick and not at all guilty, only the passing look of a man mentally patting his pockets for a thing he had been told not to mention.

"Handled," said Bog.

"Handled."

"Under management. Very secure. I'd say she's, ah," he glanced up, as if the answer were on the clouds, "in good hands. A whole lot of hands. Honestly the best hands."

He was, she realized, proud of it. That was the unforgivable part. He had no idea how it sounded.

"Nia," said Rin faintly. "Why is the Pale Murderess *handled?* Isn't she a very famous assassin? Why is the nice Val *handling* her?"

"Because he is not a nice Val," said Nia. "He is a Val who is lucky."

"Aw," said Bog.

The woman in his arms made a small noise, turned her face against his coat, and murmured something in a language that was not English. Then, quite clearly, in a sleepy stutter: "S-sorry... I'm so s-sorry, I'll... I'll fix it..." And slept again.

Bog's thumb stopped moving on her thigh. He glanced down at her, and for an instant the cheerful vacancy in his face went out like a lamp, and something much older and plainer looked through it. It was not a look Nia had ever seen on him.

"She does that," he said, more quietly. "Even asleep. She's the only one in the whole damn place who keeps trying."

Nia's eyes dropped to the hand on the thigh.

"The *hand,*" she said.

"What? Oh." He lifted it, studied it with real interest, as if it had been put there by a third party, and then very carefully, very ceremonially, transferred it to the woman's shoulder. "Better?"

"A little."

"Good. Now. About the mountain."

The gong rang a third time, and this time the sound did not roll down the ridge so much as *arrive*. The ground bucked. The purple above them swelled like a bruise and split along one edge, and out of the split, very slowly, something began to put forth a hand.

---

"Whoa," said Bog. "This is bad."

He said it the way one notes that the soup is cold. He had tipped his head all the way back, with the sleeping woman in his arms, and was studying the split in the sky with the polite attention of a customer examining a bill. "We can't fix that big one until Priya wakes up. Let's get out of here for now."

As though the word *now* had been a signal, the purple came down.

It did not fall. It *poured*, a vast glowing mass boiling out of the rift in the cloud, swelling as it came, taking on edges, a back, a spread of limbs. It was no shape Nia could name. It had the bulk of a bear and the long arms of a gibbon and, along its flanks, faces, hundreds of faces, swimming up through the light and sinking again, every one of them wide-eyed, every mouth gaping. The ridge groaned under its weight. Pebbles danced on the ledge.

"What are we supposed to *do* about that?!"

Rin was standing on the suit's shoulder with both fists in the air and her ponytail whipping in the wind. Her voice cracked. It was, Nia realized, the voice of a girl who had just worked out what the thing was *for*. "A minute ago that purple light was taking over *everything!* Animals! Trees! *Rocks!* If we leave it, Mississippi and Alabama are going to be in serious trouble! The whole Appalachian region, the whole *planet*... *Everyone's* going to get taken over, and nobody will be able to stop it!"

"Well," said Bog, "that's not really *our* problem, is it?"

A beat of silence.

"Let's just get out of here."

Nia kicked him in the shin.

She did it with the flat, practiced precision of a woman who had been wanting to do it for four days. The null-collar sucked away most of the strength behind it, but the *principle* arrived intact, and so did the toe of the boot.

"*Ow!*" said Bog.

"That is for the pod."

"I *said* I put a lot of thought in the toolkit!"

"That," said Nia, "is for what you just said."

"Nia!" Rin's voice rang out over the ledge. "Lucky Val! Get back, both of you! Get *back!* I've got this!" She was swinging herself down into the open hatch as she talked, her braid lashing, and the great machine groaned upright under her hands. "If I can't 'fix' it, I'll *clobber* it!"

She turned the suit to face the monster.

The nine-meter frame planted its feet. The cat sticker on its chest caught the light. Rin locked both steel arms in front of her, fist against fist, like a child bracing for the end of a long, long game of tag, and from somewhere deep in the guts of the machine a note began to build: a low whine, climbing, climbing, a pipe organ filling with a shining, impossible, *orange* light.

It came out of the seams of the plating. It poured up the arms, along the knuckles, in rolling bands of molten amber, and the stones under the suit's feet began to glow like coals. It was the first thing about the suit that had been orange. It was the exact color of a traffic cone and a sunset and every thing that anybody had ever painted to stand out in a crowd.

"Say, uh," said Bog, "just how far back should we go?"

He had got Priya settled over his left shoulder like a sleeping toddler. Nobody had asked him to. But the hand on her backside was not simply holding her there, which Nia noted, with the distant, clinical outrage of a woman filing an official complaint. It was *kneading*. Slowly, thoughtfully, absentmindedly, the way one kneads a loaf of bread to see if it has risen. The fingers sank into the soft, warm, brown-skinned curve of a married woman's rump, a married woman's rump, with her wedding ring still on, snoring away on his shoulder.

Then, before Nia could open her mouth, he scooped her up with the other arm and slung her over his right shoulder like a rolled-up carpet.

"*Hey!*"

"Back, back, back," said Bog cheerfully. "The lady said back."

And without the slightest shame he spread his big hand across *her* backside as well, the dark, firm, strong-muscled curve that had carried her through nine days at Baltimore, and began to knead *that*, briskly, practically, to the rhythm of his stride, as he ambled off down the slope at a pace very nearly a trot. A married woman's backside. A married woman's, with a husband in an unknown city and a ring somewhere on a chain she had not lost.

"Bog," Nia hissed into the small of his back, "I swear on every one of my ancestors that I will..."

"Uh-huh."

"...♥ ..."

She bit down on the rest of it. Her bells had begun to ring again. She considered, briefly and with real dignity, the idea of drowning in a shallow puddle of her own.

Behind them, the purple thing struck.

It came in a single titanic lunge, drawn by the roaring orange glow like a moth to a lamp. The mountain trembled. A hundred faces screamed at once.

"*It's clobberin' time!*" shouted Rin.

She let go.

The light went out of the suit in one great wave, a perfect cone of molten amber that tore across the ledge, struck the monster full in the chest, and carried it, kicking and wailing, a mile into the sky in a spray of scattered violet sparks. The thunderclap that followed rolled across three states. Every tree on the slope bent flat.

And when the dust cleared, the nine-meter suit stood alone on the ledge in a pool of cooling glass with its arms still raised, steaming, silent.

"Ha," said Rin. "Ha ha. *Ha.* Did you see?"

Then her head tipped slowly forward. A last wisp of amber light winked out of the seams. The engines coughed, spluttered, and went dark. The suit sagged on its knees and folded, with a long sigh of settling steel, into the gravel.

"...Nia?" said a very small voice from inside. "I think I'm out of fuel."

---

The ledge opened under the fallen suit like a trapdoor.

It happened without a sound. One moment the nine-meter machine was kneeling in a pool of cooling glass, steaming faintly, its pilot muttering at a dead dashboard, and the next the rock beneath it split along a black seam, and a column of purple aura came roaring up out of the crack and folded over the suit like a hand closing on a bug.

"*Rin!!*"

Nia's voice tore out of her. She thrashed on Bog's shoulder, kicked, clawed at his coat. The suit's arm was still reaching up out of the violet as the light closed over it. A glove of steel, fingers spread, as if it had been about to wave. Then it sank, and the fissure drew itself shut with a long slow *grind*, like a mouth chewing, and the ledge was just a ledge again, with a smear of glass where a girl had been.

Silence.

"Huh," said Bog, from somewhere beneath her. "That's a persistent one, isn't it?"

He let out a low, thoughtful growl.

Then his hands moved.

Both of them. In the same breath, without the slightest warning, the left to the plump brown curve riding his left shoulder and the right to the firm dark curve slung over his right, and he *kneaded*. Slowly. Warmly. With the huge, rough, green palms of a man who was not the husband of either woman, a man who had no business within a mile of either of those wedding rings, and who did it all with an air of tender, bottomless, almost reverent contentment.

"*Bog!!*" said Nia.

"What? Come on." He sounded wounded. "It's been a while. Let me enjoy it a little."

"A *girl just got swallowed by a mountain!*"

"I know. I'm *focusing.*"

Priya, on his other shoulder, made a small, drowsy noise like a kitten. She did not wake.

Bog gave both of them one last, thorough, considering squeeze, and then with a gentleness that made Nia want to scream into a pillow, he bent at the knees and set them down. Priya went first, laid out on a flat of warm stone with her head pillowed on his folded coat, sleeping the sleep of the very tired. Nia he lowered second, onto her own two feet, with one steadying hand on her elbow, as if he had been escorting her down the steps of an opera house.

"There you go."

She stood there. She was shaking. She could not have said whether it was fury.

"Anyway," said Bog, straightening, and rolling his stiff left shoulder with a crackle, "when Priya wakes up, tell her that her attendant is waiting 'down below,' and that she should come and find me. She'll know what that means. Probably. She's very sharp."

"Her *attendant.*"

"Me. Subordinate. It's in the paperwork." He was already backing toward the place where the fissure had been, flexing his knees, loosening his arms in great swinging circles, like a man warming up for a race. "Don't let her get possessed, okay? She's got a thing about that. And Nia?"

"*What?*"

He grinned over his shoulder. It was huge, broad, bright, unguarded, entirely without guile, the grin of a man who had never once been told that you did not say a thing like this to a woman in a collar.

"Love you!"

Then he ran.

He did not hesitate. He did not so much as slow. He charged across the ledge in four great strides, boots striking sparks, and without a pause or a glance at the dark seam in the stone, he threw himself headlong *into* the closing rift. For some reason, at the very last instant, he pinched his nose shut with two green fingers, like a boy about to dive off the high board into a swimming pool, and the last Nia saw of him was a pair of boot soles, a flapping coat, and a smell of cockroach skewers.

The crack shut behind him with a soft, wet *pop*.

The wind blew. Far down the mountain a tree creaked. The purple glow overhead dimmed, then flared, then settled to a low, sullen throb.

Nia stood alone on the ledge.

She stood there with her fists clenched at her sides and her bells hanging silent from every one of the eleven places they had been put. She listened to a woman snore. She counted her breaths, as she always did. *One. Two. Three.* She did not say anything. She did not say it back. She would not have said it back if he had held a knife to her.

She went on not saying it for quite some time.

"...Idiot," said Nia Okonkwo at last, to the empty mountain, and the word came out with a very small ♥ in the middle of it, all alone, where nobody could hear.

Beside her, Priya turned over in her sleep, murmured "Arjun," and went still.

---

There was nothing to be done about the suit.

It had given everything it had to one last, glorious, orange wave, and what it had left was a dead dashboard, a cold engine, and a gravity well. Rin pulled every lever she could reach. She stamped on the pedals. She begged. And the nine-meter machine, with its school-bus-less shoulder and its mismatched plating and its cat sticker, tipped over the edge of the world and fell.

"*Waaahhh!!!?*"

She fell for a long time. She hit a ledge, bounced, hit a spur of rock, bounced again, scraped a wall with a scream of metal that sent sparks across the dark, caromed off something that gave a very loud *bong*, and kept on falling, tumbling, arms and legs of the suit flailing like an upturned beetle. The purple aura, which had been trying to eat her, was rushing up past the cockpit glass in thick, glowing, boiling streams. It was somewhat like being dropped down a chimney. It was somewhat like being *held*. It buoyed her, slowed her, took a little of the sting out of each bounce, and when she finally struck the bottom, it was with a long, shuddering, groaning *crump*, like a tin can landing on a feather bed.

Silence.

Rin lay on her back in the cockpit with her goggles knocked over one eye and her mouth tasting of copper.

"...Ow," she said.

She took inventory. Head: attached. Arms: attached. Legs: also attached, and complaining. Her whole body ached, from the roots of her teeth to the soles of her feet, a deep, bruised ache like she'd been played as a drum, but nothing seemed broken. She was alive. She was, most surprisingly, intact.

Because of the cockpit.

"Make the cockpit the sturdiest part," she recited, in a deep, gravelly, gloomy voice that was most of the way to a very good impression of a certain billionaire in a cowl, "and let the surrounding structure be more fragile. That way the outer shell breaks away to dissipate the impact, and the cockpit stays safe." She stared up at the cracked glass. Then, in her own voice, small and wobbly: "Thanks, Boss."

Nobody answered.

She looked around.

The place she had landed was a vast hollow in the rock, a bottomless cavern lit from nowhere, with a faint hazy violet glow that seemed to seep out of the very stone. The ceiling was lost in dark. The walls were slick and wet and curved like the inside of a throat. She could hear water dripping somewhere far off. She could hear nothing else at all. There was no sign of an exit, no rope, no ladder, not a single scrap of daylight, and the air was thick and bleak and oppressive, as if someone had put a lid on the world.

She tried the suit again.

Levers. Switches. The little red button she had never been allowed to press. A frantic ten minutes with a wrench through the hatch of the engine bay, muttering to herself, talking to the machine as to an old friend. *Come on. Come on, girl. Don't do this to me. I know you're tired. I'm tired too.* But the great machine lay on its back with its arms folded across its chest, as stubborn and still as a stone saint, and nothing she did would make it move.

At last she gave up.

She crawled back into the cockpit, pulled the hatch down over her head, drew her knees up to her chest, and sat there in the half-dark with her arms wrapped round her shins. She did not cry. Rin did not cry. She had decided, a long time ago, when she was nine and the sky was on fire, that crying took too much of the air.

But it was *quiet*.

That was the trouble. She had not noticed how loud the world had been, until it stopped. Normally the boss would have swooped down by now, cape snapping, one gauntleted hand outstretched, to haul her up by the scruff and growl, *Don't slack off.* Or Nia, grumpy, sharp-tongued Nia, whom she had known for less than a day and had somehow, already, in the way of the very young, grown to adore, would have shown up, scolding, wanting to know *what on earth she thought she was doing.* Somebody would have come.

The silence went on for a long time.

"Hey there!"

Rin's head snapped up.

The voice was far away and far above, bright, easy, completely unbothered, and it echoed down the shaft like a stone dropped in a well. Rin slammed her palm against the hatch, forced it open an inch, then wider, and craned her neck.

Something was coming down the wall.

It was not falling. It was *bounding*, in long, loose, springy hops from one rocky outcropping to the next, with the jerky, many-limbed nimbleness of an insect crossing a ceiling. It came out of the dark in a flapping coat, tumbling and kicking and pushing off the purple streams as they rose, like a swimmer pulling himself through the sea, and as it neared she saw the big green hand waving and the broad, shameless, delighted grin.

It was Lucky Val!

"Haha, this is actually kind of fun!" He sailed past a spur of rock, caught it with one hand, spun, and dropped again, arms spread wide, hair whipping, coat flying out behind him like a cape. "Isn't it? This stuff blowing up from below, it makes you feel weightless! Like being a feather! Want to join me?"

Rin stared at him.

She was a hero, in a manner of speaking. He was a Val. He had a face like a block of green marble and shoulders like a doorframe, and there was probably a very good and well-reasoned rule somewhere about not being delighted to see him.

"...Yes," said Rin, in a very small voice, and the word came out wet and shaky and ashamed. "*Yes.* Please. Yes."

---

For a while, they frolicked.

There was no other word for it. The purple updraft that had cushioned Rin's fall rose in a broad, gentle column at the center of the cavern, and Bog, who had never in his life passed up a thing that could be jumped into, took a running start and flung himself into it with a whoop. He floated up. He floated down. He turned lazy somersaults ten meters off the ground, coat flapping, boots kicking, and when Rin, at last, after one nervous look at her dead suit, crawled out of the hatch and tried it, she shrieked with laughter all the way up and all the way down, and then demanded another go.

They raced each other to the wall and back. They played a game, invented on the spot, in which one threw a rock and the other had to catch it in midair without touching the floor. They discovered that if you clapped your hands in the right place, the cavern gave back a long, ghostly echo like a choir singing *ha, ha, ha*. Rin did her gruff impression of Darkwing. Bog did his impression of a platoon leader, a flat honking bleat that made her snort and slap her thigh. They were, it became plain, startlingly alike, two big, loose, cheerful, unworried creatures with no particular sense of danger and no earthly idea why anyone should be solemn about it.

"Okay, okay," Rin panted at last, flopping onto a heap of broken stone. "Okay. *Business.* Don't worry about a thing, Lucky Val. I'm good with machines. Seriously good. Even the boss asks me. Give me an hour and a wrench and I'll have the suit up and running, and then we'll bust out of this dump, easy as pie."

"Great," said Bog. "Sounds like a plan." He stretched, rolled his shoulders, and looked around the great dim cavern with the calm, expectant gaze of a man scanning a menu. "I'm going to take a walk. See if I can find something to eat."

Rin laughed. She laughed so hard she had to wipe her eyes on her sleeve. There was nothing to eat down here. There was nothing here at all.

"Sure!" she called. "Bring me a sandwich!"

"Will do."

He strolled off into the violet gloom, whistling, hands in his coat pockets, and Rin, still giggling, rolled over, rubbed her palms together, and went to work on the dead suit with her biggest wrench.

---

The walls of the cavern were not entirely rock.

Bog noticed this about a quarter of an hour later, when he stopped to scratch his ear and found that the stone beside him had ribs. They stood out in the wall, white and curved and enormous, sweeping up out of the rock like the timbers of a half-built ship. There were vertebrae the size of barrels. There was a skull, long and low, with a jaw like a drawbridge and a double row of teeth, half-buried in the floor. There were more beyond it. Fossils: whole herds of them, whole *epochs* of them, creatures that had lived and died in this place when the world had been wet and green and strange, laid out in the stone like a library.

"Huh," said Bog. "Didn't know you got those on this planet. Nice."

He leaned in to inspect the skull. A cheerful thought crossed his mind about whether a creature that size would have been tender. Then something moved behind him.

The purple came first, a thin, creeping, oily haze that rose out of the cracks and clung, shimmering, to the bones. It settled on the ribs, on the vertebrae, on the long white skull, and where it settled the bones *stirred*. They shivered. They rattled. They lifted off the floor and drew themselves together with a hollow, clicking, clattering sound, one fossil fusing into another, a thousand stray fragments of dead things threading into a single mighty shape, until a creature stood up in front of him that had a hundred legs, three skulls, and no particular regard for the way animals were meant to be assembled.

It opened all three mouths.

It lunged.

"Cut it out," said Bog.

He kicked it. It was a single, absent, casual kick, the sort he would have used on a stray dog that was blocking a door, and the monster went over sideways with a crash and a spray of bones. It lay there, rattling. Bog dusted off his boot.

The aura, however, did not die with it.

It lifted off the scattered bones in a slow violet curl. It hung in the air for a moment, as though thinking, as though listening, and then it turned and began to drift away across the cavern, in a long thread, out of the shadows, toward the middle of the great hollow, where a faint clanging of metal was echoing off the walls.

Bog stopped scratching his ear.

"Uh-oh," he said.

He ran.

He ran across the cavern in great long bounding strides, kicking up gravel, and he was out of breath for the first time in a week when he came round the last curve of the wall and into the open place where he had left Rin. The clanging had stopped. The wrench lay on the floor. A thin violet thread was disappearing, with the tidy finality of a snake going down a hole, into the seam of a battered, mismatched, nine-meter powered suit.

The suit was standing.

It rose slowly to its feet, with a long groan of steel and a shower of gravel, and glowing, the whole plated frame of it, with a fierce, hard, pulsing violet light that poured out of every seam and rivet and bolt-hole and made the cavern bright as noon. The cat sticker on its chest burned like a coal. Behind the cockpit glass, two eyes were shining, wide open and blazing with the same violet fire.

They were Rin's eyes.

"Oh," said Bog, in a very small voice. "This is the absolute worst."

The suit turned its head. When it spoke, the voice that came out of it was Rin's and not Rin's, doubled, and tripled, with a hollow metallic ring under it like a bell being struck in a well, a sound that scraped along the inside of the skull and set the teeth on edge.

"*It's clobberin' time!*"

And the nine-meter machine, with a roar, lowered its head and charged.

---

Bog was being driven into a corner, and it was an embarrassing feeling.

He had been in a great many fights. He had survived the front, a tank division's worth of angry superheroes, and a Val drill sergeant with a grudge, and he was fairly sure he had never been outclassed this thoroughly by a girl with a ponytail. The possessed suit hit like a falling building. One steel hand closed on his coat and *lifted* him, and he saw the cavern wall rush at his face, and the world went white and rattled. Then the other hand caught him, and he was thrown the other way. Again. A wall. A boulder. A heap of rib bones, which cracked and scattered under him like kindling. His nose burst. He tasted green blood, hot and salty, running over his lip.

"*Clobber!*" the doubled, trebled, tin-bell voice roared. "*Clobber! Clobber!*"

Another wall. His ribs gave a warning creak.

*Any more of this,* thought Bog, hanging in the air with his boots a meter from the floor, *and I'll start coming apart.*

The suit had its cockpit exposed. It was, in fact, the most exposed thing about it, a clean pane of cracked glass in the middle of its chest, with a small, soft, warm human body inside. Any Val soldier of any rank or breeding would have seen it in an instant. One fist through the glass. One squeeze. And the whole thing would stop, and the problem would be solved, and the paperwork would be minimal.

Bog looked at the glass.

He looked at the two glowing violet eyes behind it, and under them the small stubborn jaw, the oversized goggles pushed up into a lopsided ponytail, and he remembered the way she had laughed on the updraft.

*Nope,* he decided.

It was not noble. He would have been the first to admit it. Other Val might not have hesitated, but he, Bog, a sergeant of the Dominion, a provisional reservist, a man with a firm grip on his own limitations, simply could not do it. It was like being asked to eat a puppy. The thought made his stomach turn.

The suit dropped him.

He hit the floor on his hands and knees, spat a mouthful of blood, and groaned, "Right. Right. Okay." He forced one leg under him. Then the other. He rose, swaying, a great green silhouette in the glare of violet light, with his coat in rags and his fists loose at his sides.

If only he had a weapon. He had thrown the laser into a ravine. He had left the rolling pin in Florence. He patted his belt, hopefully, and his fingers brushed against something cold and smooth and curved.

The collar.

One of the two he had lifted from Hermes's shed. A heavy gray band of alien alloy, dead weight, not a weapon at all, merely a thing that had once made a great many people very sad.

*Damn it,* thought Bog. *All or nothing.*

The suit came at him again, arms spread wide. He did not back away. Instead, at the very last instant, he did a thing no sensible Val had ever done in the history of the Dominion. He *dropped*, flat on his belly, and slid. He slid under the swinging steel arms, under the pistoning knees, through a spray of gravel and a gust of purple heat, and came up beneath the great chest of the machine, inches from the glass. He put one hand on the shattered pane. He tore it away. And in the same movement, with the speed of a man who has spent six years practicing exactly this on other people's necks, he reached in and snapped the collar around Rin's throat.

*Click.*

The suit collapsed.

It did not fall so much as *fold*, in one long gasp, the lights going out in its seams, the plated limbs drooping, the great frame sagging to its knees like a puppet with its strings cut. Bog stepped back, panting, one hand pressed to his ribs.

*Success,* he thought.

But Rin came out of the cockpit.

She came out like something fired from a gun, in one long leap, her hands curled into claws and the collar gleaming at her throat, and she hit him square in the chest and bore him to the floor. Her eyes were the wrong color. Her teeth were bared. Her breath came in short shrieking gusts. She was *shaking*, he realized, head to foot, as if two very large and very angry hands were pulling her in opposite directions, and one of the hands was made of metal and the other of light. The collar was struggling to smother her. The purple was struggling to keep her. And between them was a twenty-year-old girl who weighed about as much as a sack of flour.

"*Clo... bber...*" she said, through her teeth. And then, thin and high and desperate, in her own voice, "*Lucky Val... help...*"

Bog growled low in his throat.

It was a bad contest. He could see it. The collar was strong, but it was only a collar; the purple was a hundred thousand years of dead things, and it was winning. If this went on much longer, she would not hold. Her heart would give out, or her mind would. He could *feel* the thin little bones of her wrists beginning to buckle in his grip.

*Then,* thought Bog, *I'll just have to tip the scales in the collar's favor.*

He did not know quite why he thought so. Something he had read, long ago, in the dusty back pages of a Dominion handler's manual: *in cases of collar instability, bond-calm by direct contact.* Hands, preferably. But his hands were full. He had a vague, hazy, unreliable memory that there was a second method, in a footnote, for emergencies. He was almost certain it had involved a diagram.

He rolled over, pinned her firmly, gently, face-up against the cold stone floor, with both of her wrists in one large palm and the other hand cupped behind her head so that it would not strike the rock. He looked down into her blazing violet eyes. "Sorry about this," said Bog, quite sincerely. "It's for your health."

Then he leaned in and kissed her.

It was not a long kiss. It was brief and awkward, the kiss of a man performing an unfamiliar maneuver from a manual he had half read. He held her there, with his nose bleeding down onto her chin, and counted slowly in his head to five.

The collar flared. A ring of cold white light ran round it, from the clasp to the nape and back, and for one long, aching second, nothing happened.

Then the purple *screamed*.

It came out of her mouth. It poured from her nose and her ears and the corners of her eyes in long, thin, writhing threads of violet smoke, shrieking, thrashing, tearing loose from her like a nail from a plank. It fled across the cavern in a long streak and plunged into the dark, and the light in her eyes went out. Her body went slack. Her head fell back against his palm.

Bog lifted his face.

Rin lay on the stone beneath him with her mouth open and her eyes closed, breathing, a pale thread of blood across her cheek. She looked very small.

"...Hm," said Bog, and wiped his nose with the back of his hand. "I think it worked. I think that's what the diagram was."

Her eyelids fluttered.

And then, very slowly, with a long, shuddering breath, they opened. They were brown. Wide brown eyes, bleary and bewildered, staring up at a bleeding green face a hand's breadth from her own.

"...Lucky Val?" said Rin, in a very small voice. "Did you just kiss me?"

---

"Well," said Bog, with the air of a man explaining a coupon, "let's just say that I introduced a second squatter into your body, let the two of them fight it out, and then threw my support behind one of them."

"...What?"

"The collar. It's a tenant, basically. A very grumpy tenant."

Rin stared up at him. Her brown eyes were slowly clearing, the pupils shrinking to pinpricks, and a flush was climbing up her throat above the cold gray band of the collar. She opened her mouth to say something, probably several things, and then her whole body went rigid.

A thin violet thread slid out from the cavern wall.

It crept across the floor in a long smooth curve, quiet as a snake, and curled around her ankle. Another followed it, and another, soft as breath, fine as hair. They wound up her calves, her thighs, the line of her ribs. A shimmer of violet bloomed under her skin, and her eyes flickered, lost focus, and for a moment went fierce again.

"Damn," said Bog, glumly. "Seems like doing things halfway just doesn't work."

He loomed over her once more. He was a very large man, and in the purple half-light, with a blood-streaked chin and a ruined coat, he looked rather like a landslide that had decided to take an interest. Rin swallowed.

It was a small, ordinary, stubborn sound. And then, because she was Rin, and she had been given a choice, small as it was, in a very bad moment, she lifted her chin, and, with her teeth chattering and her fingers white-knuckled on his coat, she said:

"...The Boss... is going to be *furious*..."

"Probably," said Bog.

"...But if it's the only way..." She shut her eyes. She was twenty years old, a grown woman, an orphan of the war, a hero with a lopsided ponytail and no more fear of an angry man than of a rainstorm, and her voice, when it came, was small and fierce and perfectly steady. "Then stop talking and *do it*, Lucky Val. I'm not afraid."

"That's the spirit," said Bog.

He bent his head.

What followed took a long time, and the cavern, being a cavern, kept its opinions to itself.

It began with her breath. It caught, once, in a small shaken gasp, like a door opening in the wind, and then it came again, and again, faster, lighter, higher, until it broke on a sound she had not known she could make, sweet and breathless and astonished. The collar at her throat flared. A ring of cold white fire ran round and round it, flickering with each moan, and the hands that had been tearing at his coat came loose and slid, trembling, around his neck.

The purple came for her. It was stubborn. It tried to coil tighter, to smother, to sink in through every pore, and Rin cried out, *"Ah!"* and arched against the floor. But the white fire answered. It shone brighter, hotter, steadier with each gasp, driving the violet back from her skin, from her hair, from the hollow of her throat, in great shuddering waves, a tide going out, and her laughter, when it came, was half a sob and half a song.

"*B-Boss... is going to...* ah... *kill* you..."

"I'll be sure to send him a card," said Bog, against her ear.

But the purple was not done. It surged back, shrieking, and for a moment the whole cavern seemed to lean over them, to bend like a great ear. Faces flickered in the walls. A thousand pale mouths opened. Bog felt the cold pressure on the back of his neck, and the thin, small, trembling body beneath him went stiff in his arms, and its breath hitched, and its eyes rolled up and showed their whites.

He lifted his head.

There was nothing for it. He had done his best at gentleness. It had taken an hour, and a great deal of blood, and a lot of poor, bad poetry. It had not been *enough*.

"All right," said Bog, softly, to the girl, and to the walls, and to everything that listened in the dark. "I'll have to make it perfectly clear just who exactly you belong to."

His hand closed on the collar's clasp. The white fire blazed up, hard and clean as a star, and the collar, at the pull of its handler's grip, rang a single bright note that rolled out across the underground sea.

High above, on a ledge of warm stone, under a coat that smelled of cockroach skewers, a woman in a small crimson costume frowned in her sleep, and stirred.

---

Somewhere in the course of it, Rin's voice began to fill with hearts.

She did not notice at first. She had been saying very ordinary things, *wait* and *no* and *that's not fair*, in her ordinary loud cheerful voice, and then, between one word and the next, they began to turn. A little ♥ crept into the gap between *wait* and *Lucky*. Another slipped into *not fair*, and bloomed, and stayed. By the time she heard herself, they were everywhere, rounding every vowel and softening every consonant, as though the words themselves had gone warm.

"L-Lucky Val♥... that's... that's cheating♥..."

"Probably," said Bog.

He had sat up against a boulder, crossing his legs on the cool stone, and gathered her up onto his lap as easily as lifting a cat. She straddled him with her knees on either side of his thighs, and he held her there with both big hands, and bounced her, gently, in a slow, rocking rhythm like a boat riding a swell.

It was Nia's favorite.

He knew that. He had learned it on the couch, in the long week when he had not been able to stop whispering. She would never have said so, not if you had pulled out her teeth, but she would cling to him with both arms and bury her face in his neck, and all her fierce sharp edges would soften, and her whole body would go quiet in a way that said *safe*, *safe*, *safe*. It was, he had decided privately, the best thing he knew.

He had tried it on Rin, and it worked.

She clung. Her arms went tight around his neck, and her face pressed into the hollow of his shoulder, and a long shuddering sigh ran through her from head to foot. Her breath came in short soft bursts against his skin. The white fire in the collar steadied, brightened, and the last threads of violet, which had been creeping stubbornly up her spine, shriveled and fell away.

The cavern gave a long, grudging groan. Something far off, in the dark, went quiet.

"Well," said Bog, thoughtfully, to the top of her head. "The third consort position is open."

Rin went rigid.

It was only for a moment. Her fingers dug into the back of his coat, and her whole slight body flinched, and she lifted her flushed face from his shoulder and stared up at him, wide-eyed, with the unfocused shine of a girl who has been flung out of one dream into another.

"The... the *third*...?" Her voice shook. It was a small voice, a very small one, with hearts hanging off the ends of it. She swallowed. "D-Do I have the right to refuse that...♥?"

"Hmm." Bog considered the question with the gravity he brought to all paperwork. "No."

He settled his hands on her. He took firm hold of her small round backside, one cheek in each broad green palm, and kneaded it slowly, thoroughly, possessively, with the unhurried air of a man testing a loaf. Rin made a sound.

It was a very small sound. It was not a protest.

She hid her face in his neck again, and she held on tighter, and the silence stretched out between them, warm and dim and heavy. She was twenty years old. She was a hero, in her way. She had a mentor who would be furious, and a suit that would not move, and a collar that would not come off, and a dreadful, secret, lopsided little feeling inside her, like a stone dropped in a well, that did not feel even slightly like a prison.

"...Third?" she mumbled, at last, into his coat. "What about... first? Who's... who's first♥?"

"Hm?" said Bog. "Oh. Nia."

There was a pause.

Rin's head came up so fast she nearly broke his chin.

"...*Nia?!*"

---

The kiss that followed was a very long one, and Rin, to her own surprise, did not spend it fighting.

It was an unhurried, unembarrassed kiss, with none of the awkward counting of the first, and when it ended she was breathing in small shaken sips, with her forehead against his chin, and the collar at her throat shone a soft steady white. The last of the violet had gone. The cavern was quiet and ordinary and slightly damp. Only the heartbeat in her ears was loud.

"Rin," said Bog, in a low, confidential murmur, as if the two of them were sharing a bag of chips behind a bar. "Listen. I'm going to tell you something, and you can't tell Nia. It's still a secret from her."

"...♥ Okay...♥"

"The plan is, she leaves her husband, essentially, and becomes my wife. Properly. With a house, and a garden, and the whole bit. And then she bears me a lot of children." He nodded gravely, as if announcing a quarterly budget. "Many. It's the main thing. It's the whole reason I'm here."

Rin lifted her head.

Her eyes were enormous. They had gone round and bright and slightly watery, the eyes of a girl who has just been handed the plot of a very long and complicated comic and told she is in the next issue. "That's... that's *so* much," she whispered. "Does Nia know she's...?"

"I'm working on it."

"Is she going to *kill* you?"

"Probably, once the collar's off. That's already in the deal." He smiled. "So. Rin. Would *you* be willing to have my children, too?"

She opened her mouth.

She closed it again.

It was, she supposed, a ridiculous question. It was a mad question. She was twenty years old and an orphan, with a cat sticker and a school-bus door and a mentor in a cape, and she had known this enormous green creature for the better part of a day, and in all of that time nobody had asked her anything half so large. Nobody had ever asked her to be *part* of something. The boss kept her close, but never *in*, a small bright dog at the heel of a man who had already lost everybody else.

And Bog, being Bog, and a Val, and a man who had never once in his life understood that a question could be anything but a question, waited. He did not look away. He did not smirk. He simply held her, solid and warm and patient, and waited for the answer.

He did, however, also decide, with the instinct of his kind, to underline that the matter was already settled.

He did it without any warning at all. He moved, in one slow deep roll of his hips, and the part of him that had been buried quietly inside her all this while rose, firm and unhurried and certain, and nudged her cradle upward, like a man knocking politely on a door that he fully intended to walk through.

"*Ah!*"

Rin's whole body arched. Her head fell back. Her fingers sank into his coat, and a sound came out of her that was not any word, a high, startled, helpless cry with a ♥ trembling at the end of it, that echoed off the cavern walls and came back, three times, with every echo softer and sweeter than the one before.

"That," said Bog kindly, "was a yes, right?"

"...♥ Y-You... you can't just... *ask* it like that♥...!"

"I did ask."

"You asked and then you... you *did*♥...!"

"Efficient," Bog agreed.

Rin buried her flaming face in his neck. She was shaking, and not entirely from fear. She was laughing, a little, in the way you laugh when the roller coaster has already left the platform and there is no use arguing with the cars. She said something into his collar that he could not hear.

"Hm?"

"...I said," mumbled Rin, "if I'm going to be the third one... I get to name the first kid."

"*Deal,*" said Bog, delighted, and sealed it.

---

Far above, on a ledge of warm stone under the shadow of a mountain, a woman in a small crimson costume drew a long slow breath, and opened her eyes.

They were brown. They were wide, and a little frightened, and entirely her own.

A tall, dark-skinned woman in borrowed overalls was kneeling beside her, with the quiet, dreadful patience of a rehab nurse.

"Priya," said Nia Okonkwo, "I think it's time you and I had a talk."
