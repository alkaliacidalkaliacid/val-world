# THE ASHEN DOMINION
## Bog & Nia

*A side story of the Val World*

---

## Chapter 1: The Black Market

Bog ambled through the black market with his thumbs hooked in his belt, the way a man walks through his own backyard.

The market had taken over the bones of an old multistory car park. Cables ran down from the ceiling in loose, sagging bundles, tapped from some Dominion relay that nobody would admit to knowing about, and the stolen current made the strings of bulbs overhead flicker and hum. Under them, the ash-gray light of the afternoon barely reached the stalls. The air was thick with frying oil, scorched metal, and the sweet chemical reek of something being distilled in a bucket. Boots and bare feet had worn a track through the grit, and a thousand voices haggled over it in six languages.

Everything was for sale. Crates of ration bars still stamped with Dominion glyphs sat beside piles of salvaged phones that would never ring again. A boy sold "genuine Val armor plating," which was clearly a hubcap. Real Dominion supplies were stacked behind a tarp, the kind the Elites said must never pass into human hands. Bog gave them a long look and a shrug. The Elites said a lot of things. Besides, the profit was good, the thrill was better, and nobody had ever gotten a medal for being a boring soldier.

There were drug tables where the vendors didn't even bother to look at him, because nothing they sold had any effect on a Val. There was illicit booze, which most certainly did. Bog had learned that the hard way and still remembered the morning after. In the back, in cages draped with oilcloth, something clicked and chittered. It might have come from another world, or from a supervillain's basement. Either way, the Elites had forbidden it, which of course was why it drew the biggest crowd.

Then there was the stretch of the market he usually avoided. The women were kept in rows there, and they were, by an absurd margin, the healthiest things for sale in the city. Everyone knew why. The Elites protected them, so they were fed and watered better than half the garrison. It showed. Their eyes were bright with fury, and every one of them tracked him as he passed. The first one raised a middle finger. The next did too, then the whole row, a ripple of defiance down the line. Bog grunted. They were the most ill-tempered merchandise in the market, and he only wanted to walk through that row on days when he felt ready to wrestle a superhero and win. Today was not that day.

He slipped into the crowd instead. On his left, a man in a filthy coat was shouting at the ceiling, certain that judgment was coming for the invaders and that every tooth in every green head would be counted. On his right, a woman in white robes was singing about the Val as humanity's saviors, her voice rising to drown him out. They stood a few feet apart and glared at the sky, not at each other. Bog passed between them like a man between two radios on different stations.

Earth had grown on him. He had stopped missing the homeworld somewhere around year three, and now he was thinking, seriously, about a house. A real one, with walls. His comrades had started buying slaves, mostly for chores, and eventually for the other thing the Elites cared about, the thing that no Val had ever managed on his own. He'd have to swear the oath, of course, before the registrar and the glowing seal, to keep the human healthy and well. The Elites were strict about that. Bog had a heavy hand and a talent for breaking furniture, so he would have to choose someone sturdy.

But that was a decision for another day.

A skewer stand steamed at the end of the aisle. The old man behind it was turning something long and dark and glossy over the coals, and as Bog leaned closer, a leg curled in the heat.

A giant cockroach.

"Ew," Bog said.

He pulled out a coin and held it out anyway.

## Chapter 2: The Ultimatum

Grilled cockroach skewers, it turned out, tasted pretty good. Smoky, a little sweet, with a crunch like fried shrimp. Bog was licking the last of the glaze off his thumb when the communicator on his forearm began to flash.

It was his platoon leader.

"Enjoying your leave, Bog?" The voice crackled through the speaker, as gravelly as ever. "If you want to stay on Earth and join the reserves, you've got three days to buy a breeding pair and do your duty to the Dominion. Otherwise, the military police will hunt you down and ship you off to the Tau Ceti system. Nothing there but one giant bog of a planet. The Elites say the locals evolved from brittle stars, and, get this, they're supposedly compatible with us."

The line went dead.

Bog stood very still, the empty skewer still in his hand. Behind him, the preacher and the cultist were shouting at each other again.

"A bog," he muttered. "Of course it's a bog."

## Chapter 3: The Purchase

Bog headed back to the slave market. A Val merchant had set up a row of cubic containment fields, each humming faintly, and in each one a woman was held crouched, her back to the aisle. Their hands were raised above them in the only gesture they had left, and every one of them was giving the world the finger.

"Looking to buy?" the merchant asked, spreading his hands over the row like a man presenting fine wine. "Which one catches your eye?"

"One that won't bite me, or cut my throat while I sleep," Bog said.

"Timid, aren't you?" The merchant snorted. "Expect to lose an eye or two. Regenerative treatment grows them right back."

"Forget it. A guy in my platoon had tentacles come out of his eye sockets after one of those treatments."

"Sounds useful to me."

He walked down the row, giving each field a sharp slap as he passed. Every slap drew a furious noise from inside, and the bells on the captives chimed with every movement. The curses that followed came out sweet and breathless, with a small, shameful tremor under the anger, and a few were punctuated by hearts. The merchant seemed to find that charming.

"Basic domestication's done on all of them," he said. "They won't tear your throat out. They might leave a few bite marks."

Bog gave him a flat look. "I need one that won't break, even under a Val's strength."

"Then maybe learn to hold back a little."

It sounded exactly like something his platoon leader would say. Bog scowled and flicked one of the bells, then another, listening to the chimes ring out over the moans and the insults. Most of the women snarled back right away. One only growled, a low, stifled sound with nothing sweet in it at all.

"What's this one?"

"A real find. A Bound."

"A Bound?" Bog's brows went up. "Odd to see one in a backwater market like this."

"Watch it!"

"Eh. Whatever."

"Secondhand," the merchant admitted. "She was passed around the front-line barracks for a while, and she's still got a rebellious streak. The other Bounds learned obedience a long time ago. They're all sitting in the Elites' laps now."

Bog snorted.

"Is that so? From where I'm standing, it looks like the Elites are the ones who got conquered. Now they're the ones doing whatever the Bounds tell them."

The merchant gave the thick, dark-skinned backside a proprietary pat. "This one's the Black Huntress. Stubborn, supposedly because she's got a human mate and cubs somewhere, and she's still fiercely loyal to them."

The name rang a bell. Wasn't she the one who had wiped out an entire tank division? An old classmate from the academy had been in that division, and nobody had heard from him since. So she was stubborn, and on top of that she was loyal to some particular pack. A family, the humans called it.

"What a hassle," Bog muttered.

The merchant was ready for that. "Listen, I heard this from a trader I met on one of the worlds we conquered. He dealt in the flying lizards native there, everything from taming to selling. He had a saying. If you want top-tier winged stock on a lizard ranch, catch a superior wild female, ideally one that already has a mate and offspring. She'll fight hardest because of her loyalty, but once she's tamed, she'll give you that same devotion, maybe more. And her breeding record's already proven."

"Doesn't really resonate with me," Bog said. He couldn't deny, though, that she looked sturdy. Hips like an ammunition crate, shoulders that didn't belong in a cube, and calves like cured rope.

He sighed, paid the man, and hoisted her onto his shoulder. She stayed docile while the containment field was still up, and she stayed docile for exactly as long as it took the field to drop.

Then she twisted in his grip, quick as a whip, and drove a knee at his face. It was aimed to cave in his nose and push the bone straight into his brain.

Bog caught most of it on the cheek. Blood began to drip from his nose.

*Wait. Wasn't the collar supposed to suppress her powers? Wasn't she supposed to be pre-tamed?*

Apparently, though, she had enough raw strength left to make a Val bleed even without any powers. Bog wiped his nose with the back of his hand, looked at the red smear, and shrugged.

"Well, she *is* sturdy."

He strolled off toward the car park, carrying her on his shoulder like a sack of grain while she swore at the back of his armor. His aircar waited on the lowest level, and a gang of ragged orphans was crouched around it. They were disabling the security system, and by the looks of it they had gotten surprisingly far. The next stage, however, would have set off the anti-theft charge and burned all of them to death.

Bog flicked a handful of coins at their feet. "Scram."

The orphans scattered, scooping up the coins as they ran. He dumped his cargo in the back seat, strapped her in, and slammed the door.

Through the glass, she screamed a curse at him, one of the genuinely effective kind. Bog felt it land somewhere between his shoulder blades and stay there.

## Chapter 4: Intake

Bog's home in the Wasteland was a squat, repurposed relay station on the edge of a dead freeway, and he'd barely shouldered through the door before he propped the Huntress against the wall and aimed his wrist-projector at her. The holo-photo flickered, froze, and fed into the reserve application. He submitted it before he could change his mind.

The reply came while he was still pulling off his boots. *Provisionally accepted.* Bog let out a breath that went on a bit too long. The Tau Ceti posting dissolved from his schedule, and with it the vision of a planet that was all bog, which struck him as a terrible joke.

"All right," he said, turning to her. "Intake."

She stared at him with an expression that could have cracked concrete.

"Kneel. Sit back on your heels. Hands behind your head, and look at me."

For a moment he thought she'd refuse. Her jaw was tight enough that the muscle bunched under her scar, and the collar flickered at her throat as if waiting for an excuse. Then she complied, slowly, with her teeth gritted the whole way down. Maybe the merchant hadn't lied about the training. The bells at her chest and hips gave one clear, sweet chime as she settled, and the sound, so lovely and so wrong, seemed to make her flinch more than the collar ever had.

Bog cleared his throat and tried to look like he knew what he was doing. He had read the registrar's manual twice. It had not covered this.

"Profile," he said. "'Black Huntress' can't be all of it. Names, history, everything. Your pack, where you were raised, what you've done. I'm your owner, so I have a right to know."

She growled. It was a low sound, almost a purr in its menace. Then she began to talk.

"My name is Nia Okonkwo." Her voice was flat and rough, an instrument that had been used as a weapon for years. "I was born in Maryland, raised in a three-room apartment above a laundromat, and I woke up one night when the building next door was on fire and there was a kid in a window. I didn't think. I just went. That's the whole origin story, you overgrown pickle."

"Pickle," Bog repeated, and filed it away.

"I was a physical therapist's assistant. I taught old men to walk again after their strokes. I was better at that than I was at hitting things, and I was *very* good at hitting things." Her eyes didn't move from his. "I held the Baltimore corridor for nine days. Thirty thousand people walked out through it. You can put that in your little form."

Bog entered it, laboriously, on the projector.

"My pack," she went on, and the word dripped with contempt, "is none of your damn business. They're alive. They're somewhere you'll never find them. And if you so much as look in their direction, I'll rip your spine out of your back and use it to hang the curtains."

"That's not very helpful for the form."

"Good."

He watched her for a moment. She was shaking, though not from fear. She held herself like a woman bracing against a flood. Somewhere in the pit of his gut, something shifted uncomfortably.

"Right," Bog said at last, and turned off the projector. "Well. That's... a start."

She spat a word at him in a language he didn't recognize. Whatever it meant, the way it landed made the bells hum.

"How many?" Bog asked. He kept his eyes on the form. "At the front. How many of us did you, ah, comfort?"

Her jaw worked. The silence stretched until the bells at her hips trembled with the effort of her holding still.

"Four hundred and twelve," Nia said at last. She said it the way a medic reads out a casualty count, flat and exact, each word bitten off. "I counted every one. I'll keep counting until I can start settling the account."

Bog blinked. He did the arithmetic twice, and it still didn't come out right.

"Four hundred and twelve," he repeated, "and not one of them took?" He looked up, honestly baffled. "The Elites would pay a fortune for that kind of record. The cradles have been dying for years, and you're telling me you walked through half a regiment and came out empty?"

"You think a womb is a vending machine, you green idiot?" Her lip curled back from her teeth. "My people have a saying. The spirits only open the door for someone worthy. They watched every one of you come and go, and not one of you got past the threshold." Her eyes found his, and they were very steady. "That's the real reason the Elites want me, isn't it? They're desperate, and the door won't open for them. And you won't be the one it opens for either. I promise you that."

Bog opened his mouth to argue, found nothing in it, and closed it again.

"And since you're so interested in my record," Nia went on, her voice dropping into something almost conversational, "let me tell you what happens when this collar comes off. I start with the knees. I take them one at a time, slowly, so you have time to appreciate each one. Then the elbows. By the time I get to the neck, you'll be asking me to hurry." She tilted her head. "And then I'll walk out of here and find the next one. And the next. Four hundred and twelve, Bog. I've got a lot of work ahead of me."

The bells chimed softly as she settled back on her heels, hands still locked behind her head, glaring at him with an unbowed patience that made the hair on his arms rise.

Bog put down the projector and went to find a drink. Whatever the Elites had been warning him about, it wasn't this.

## Chapter 5: The Lemon Apron

Bog had barely been listening. He was busy taking inventory of his own kitchen with some pride.

"Everything in here is Earth-made," he said, gesturing with the beer he'd just fished out of the fridge. "Canned goods, pots, pans, the whole kitchen. Even the refrigerator. I did tinker with the power system a bit, because the wiring was ridiculous, but it works. So." He waved a hand toward the stove. "Cook something for me."

The curses that followed were long, inventive, and mostly anatomical. Bog let them wash over him like weather.

"I'm not cooking in *this*," Nia finished, jerking her chin at herself. Her whole body was a study in rage. She was bare except for the collar and the small, bright, chiming devices.

Bog considered this. He rummaged in a drawer and held up an apron, a faded blue one with a chipped print of a smiling lemon.

Her eyes went wide. She snatched it out of his hand, yanked it on, and stomped toward the kitchen with the look of a woman who would have preferred to punch a hole through the floor and keep going. She got three steps. Then the bells chimed, her stride faltered, and the hardware caught her at the hips and pulled her up short. She staggered, caught the doorframe, and stood there trembling, breathing hard.

"Sorry," Bog said, with no idea what for. "They do that."

She started again, slower, in a stiff, controlled stride that rang out like a procession of tiny, furious church bells.

Bog dropped onto the couch, which was also Earth-made and comfortable beyond all reason. He cracked the beer and listened to the music. It was lovely, honestly. The chiming moved around the kitchen as she opened cupboards, slammed them, and swore at the stove. The apron only covered the front, but she kept her back straight and refused to hurry, and to Bog it looked like the first real thing he'd seen on this planet.

"Shame about your mate," he remarked, entirely without malice. He was simply a Val, thinking aloud. "Never getting to see this again."

She went rigid.

*My husband,* Nia thought, in letters a mile high. *My only husband would never have made his wife dress like this, you perverted pickle.*

She said none of it. She cracked an egg into the pan with a controlled, precise snap, and held her tongue between her teeth until it hurt. Four hundred and twelve, she reminded herself. And this one was only the start.

She was not going to let this alien win.

## Chapter 6: Under the Table

**Schlurp-schlurp-schlurp. "...I've ever..." Slop. Sloosh. "...encountered..." Slrp, slrp, slrp.**

*Don't gag. Don't you dare gag. That's what he wants. Breathe through the nose, you've done harder things than this.*

*Breathe. Count.*

*Four hundred and thirteen.*

*Sweet spirits, it just keeps coming. How much does one of these creatures hold? It's like being hosed down by a fire engine. Don't think about the taste. Think about Baltimore. Think about the corridor, nine days, thirty thousand people walking out under a sky full of fire. You held that line. You can hold this.*

*That hand in my hair. He's patting me. Like a dog. Like a good girl who fetched the paper.*

*Let him. Let him pat. Every touch is a note in the ledger, and I never forget a debt.*

*Daniel would laugh at that. He'd say, "Nia, only you would audit your own captors." He'd say it in that soft, tired voice after a double shift, with his shoes already off and his thumb rubbing the scar on my cheek. And Amara would roll her eyes, and Chidi would ask if the bad man had a name.*

*Bog. His name is Bog. Remember it. Like a swamp. Like what he's going to be buried in.*

*It's over. It's finally over, and I didn't choke, and I didn't give him my voice. He got my mouth. He can have it. The spirits know where I actually live.*

*Swallow. Swallow, and keep your face still. Don't let him see the tears. They're not for him. They're just water, and they'll dry.*

*Somewhere out there my family is alive, and they're waiting. I'm coming back to them.*

*And this one will not be coming with me.*

*Look at it. A thread of spit, hanging off my lip like a spider's silk, and I can't even wipe it away because my hands are locked behind my head.*

*Don't look away. Don't give him the satisfaction of shame. This isn't mine to be ashamed of. It's his. Every drop of it belongs to him, and one day I'll hand it all back with interest.*

*It catches the light. It's almost pretty. That's the worst of it, how everything in this house is made to look pretty. The bells, the collar, the lemon apron, the music of my own body being used against me.*

*He's staring. He's thinking it's beautiful. He's a child who found a toy and thinks it loves him.*

*Let him think it.*

*Daniel, if you could see me, you wouldn't see this. You'd see the woman who walked into a burning building because a kid was in the window. You'd see the one who held a corridor for nine days. This is a thread. Threads break.*

*So will the collar. So will he.*

*Breathe. Wait. Remember Baltimore. Remember your children's names.*

*Amara. Chidi. Amara. Chidi.*

*I'm still here.*

---

*Good girl.*

*Four hundred and twelve men, and not one of them ever said that. They called me plenty of things. Not that. That's a word for a dog that has finally stopped biting.*

*His hand is warm. That's the part I can't forgive. I want it to be cold, rough, cruel, something I can hate cleanly. It's almost gentle, as if he thinks he's being kind. As if he believes this is how a decent owner treats his property.*

*That's how it works, isn't it? That's how they get you to the end of the road. Not with the pain. Pain I can survive. It's the small, soft moments, the pat on the head and the "good girl," so you almost forget what you are supposed to be.*

*I won't forget.*

*I am Nia Okonkwo. I am a mother. I am the woman who held Baltimore. I am not a good girl, and I am not yours.*

*Pat my head all you like, Bog. Pat it while you can. Every kindness you hand me is a debt, and I'm the most patient creditor you'll ever meet.*

*Look at him, half-asleep, one hand on my hair, his beer going warm on the table. He thinks it's over. He thinks I've learned.*

*Learn this instead.*

*The door hasn't closed. The spirits are still listening. And the next time this hand reaches for my head, I will be counting its fingers.*

---

The kiss was dry and brief, the kind a man gives a child who has finished her homework. It landed on her forehead and lingered a second longer than it needed to.

*You did well.*

Nia's breath caught. She felt it happen, a small stumble in her chest, and she hated it more than she had hated anything in the last six years.

*No. Not this. Anything but this.*

*In the barracks, nobody said a word. Not "good," not "well done," not even an insult worth remembering. They took what they wanted and left, and I stared at the ceiling and counted the cracks. Four hundred and twelve men and not one of them looked at my face. I was furniture. I was weather. I was a thing that happened to them.*

*And this idiot, this soft, stupid, lemon-apron-handing pickle, kisses my forehead and tells me I did well.*

*It's a trick. It has to be. It's the oldest one there is. Starve a person of kindness long enough, then hand her a crumb, and watch her follow you home.*

*But he doesn't know it's a trick. That's the horror of it. He means it. He thinks he's being kind.*

Her eyes stung. She fixed them on the table leg and let her vision blur, because tears were water and water dried.

*Daniel used to kiss my forehead like that. After the long shifts. When I came home with blood under my nails and couldn't talk about it.*

*No. You don't get to borrow that. You don't get to put your mouth where his went.*

*I am Nia Okonkwo. I am not grateful. I am not confused. I am a woman who is going to take this collar off and make a very long list of the things I'm owed.*

The bells chimed once as she swallowed.

*Amara. Chidi. Amara. Chidi.*

*Hold the line.*

---

*Wife.*

*He said it like he was reading a name off a menu he'd always wanted to try.*

*Kind-hearted. Cherished. Those are Daniel's words, the ones he whispered over Chidi's crib at three in the morning, when I was too tired to be anything but soft. And now this thing is wearing them like stolen clothes.*

*Don't flinch. He's watching for it. Every kiss is a test, every stroke of my hair a probe, and he is checking where the wall gives. He doesn't even know that's what he's doing. That's what makes him so dangerous.*

*He thinks the others were the villains. The ones in the barracks, the "foolish Vals." As if he's a different species from them. As if there's a version of this where the collar is a ring and the leash is a vow.*

*He bought me. He hung bells on me. He told me to crawl beneath his table. And in the same breath he's calling me kind.*

*Listen to him, Nia. Listen to what's underneath. He isn't lying. He believes it. He actually believes that if he's gentle enough, I'll forget which of us is holding the leash.*

*That's the whole trick. They don't need to break you. They just need you to start flinching less.*

*Another kiss. Another word. "Wife."*

*I'm not your wife. I'm not your good girl. I'm not your Bound. I'm a mother of two, and one day my husband will see my face again.*

*Say it again, Bog. Say it a hundred more times. Say it until it's the only thing you remember.*

*Because when the collar comes off, you will hear it in your sleep.*

*Hold the line.*

---

The soap was Earth-made, sharp with lavender, and Bog used far too much of it. He worked in careful, clumsy circles, as if he were afraid she might dissolve. He brushed her teeth with a toothbrush that vanished in his fist, humming something tuneless, and rinsed her with water he'd warmed himself.

Then he didn't lock her in a cube. He carried her to the bed instead, a real one, with a mattress and a sagging blanket, and lay down with her tucked against his chest.

*Where is the field? Where's the cage?*

*He's holding me. His arm is across my ribs, and it weighs more than my whole body used to feel like. I could count his heartbeats. Slow. Heavy. Like a drum buried under a hill.*

*He's smaller than I thought. No. I'm smaller. I hadn't noticed. My whole body fits inside his arms, all the muscle, all the years, every bruise. Six years of training, and I'm a child curled against a wall.*

"Good girl," Bog murmured into her hair, again and again, a low rumble she could feel through her back. "Good girl. Good girl."

*Stop saying it. Stop saying it. Stop.*

*Soap and lavender. Clean sheets. A mattress. I haven't slept on one since the corridor. The last thing I smelled that wasn't ash and sweat was Chidi's hair, the night before the evacuation, and he squirmed when I kissed him and said, "Mom, you're squishing me."*

*Don't. Don't go there. You'll cry, and he'll hear it.*

*He's not even trying to take anything. That's what I can't understand. He bathed me like I was a sick animal he'd rescued from a ditch. Nothing in him wants to hurt me right now.*

*But he bought me. He hung the bells. He is still the one who decides when I eat, when I sleep, when I'm washed.*

*Kind and cruel. Gentle and monstrous. Both of them wearing the same face.*

*This is the most dangerous thing in this house. Not the collar, and not the bells. This.*

*The warm bed. The heartbeat. The lullaby that isn't even a song.*

*I'm so tired.*

*Amara. Chidi. Amara. Chidi.*

*Daniel's thumb on my scar.*

*Hold the line.*

*Hold—*

Her breathing slowed. Her hands, tucked against her chest where he'd left them, loosened.

Beside her, the bells gave one small, silver chime, and then nothing.

## Chapter 7: The Routine

1. *Shlup. Shlup-shlup.* "You overgrown pickle, you smell like a swamp and you've got the table manners of one too." *Glurp.*

2. *Slrp! Slop.* "Four hundred and twelve men before you, and you're still the ugliest." *Slop-slop.*

3. *Schlurp-schlurp-schlurp.* "Is this the lemon apron's fault, or are you always this proud of yourself?" *Sloosh.*

4. *Slurrrp...* "Tau Ceti would have been an upgrade. At least the brittle stars have taste." *Shlup.*

5. *Gulp-slurp.* "Go ahead, tell me I'm a good girl again. I'll wait. I've got all day to hate you." *Slrp, slrp, slrp.*

6. *Sluuurp!* "You hold your beer like a man holding a grudge. At least the beer's got something going for it." *Glurp.*

7. *Shlup. Slop.* "You couldn't hold a candle to the dumbest man in Baltimore, and he's been dead six years." *Chime. Slop-slop.*

8. *Slurrrp... shlup.* "Your Elites can't even find the leak in their own cradles. And you think *I'm* the broken one?" *Gulp-slurp.*

9. *Schlurp. Slrp. Slop.* "Pat my head one more time. Go on. I'm counting every finger, Bog, and I'm very good at arithmetic." *Sloosh.*

10. *Glurp-glurp-glurp.* "Stinker. Rougher and uglier than any soldier I've ever met. And the absolute worst." *Sluuurp! Gulp-slurp.*

*Don't stop. Say something. Anything. You were mid-sentence, you had the next insult loaded, and now there's nothing, just my own throat closing like a fist, and my breath coming ragged where he can hear it.*

*He's heard it. Of course he has. His hand stopped moving in my hair.*

*Don't let it be a tremor. Call it anger. Call it the collar. Call it anything but what it is.*

*There's that thread again, hanging between my lip and him, shining like something precious. I pulled away and it came with me, long and thin and stubborn, and it doesn't break. Even that won't let go of me.*

*Look at it. Six years ago I would have laughed at this. The woman who carried a collapsing apartment building on her back, kneeling on a kitchen floor with a lemon on her chest, losing a fight against her own windpipe.*

*Stop. That's the wrong story. I'm not losing. I've kept the count. I've kept the names. I've kept every insult I owe him, and I'm still counting.*

*So why did my voice break?*

*Not from fear. I know what fear feels like, and this isn't it. It's the routine. Three times, four, five times a day, and the strange, awful truth is that my body has stopped bracing for the blow. It waits for the pat on the head instead.*

*That's what's trembling. Not my courage. My expectation.*

*He's watching me with those big, stupid, patient eyes. He isn't angry that I stopped. He's worried. The overgrown pickle is worried about me.*

*Don't let him see that you noticed.*

*Wipe your face. Straighten your back. Find the next word, the sharpest one you've got, and put it in your mouth like a blade. You'll hate him tomorrow too. You have to. If you stop hating him, there's nobody left holding the corridor.*

*Amara. Chidi. Daniel.*

*Breathe.*

*"Stinker," I'll say. "Rougher and uglier than any soldier I've ever met."*

*Just as soon as my voice comes back.*

## Chapter 8: The Deal

## Chapter 9: Eco-Friendly

## Chapter 13: A Day in the Life of the Black Huntress

**9:00 AM.** She wakes before Bog, as she has every morning since the second week. The arm across her ribs weighs more than her old body ever did. She counts the stains on the ceiling (Maryland, still), slides out from under it an inch at a time, and the bells give her away anyway. "Mmh. Morning, wife," he mumbles. "Not yet," she says, and goes to start the kettle.

**9:30 AM.** Breakfast. The kitchen is still a crime scene, but she has spent a week rearranging it in secret, one drawer a day, and Bog has noticed nothing. Egg, rice, a tin of Earth beans that expired before the war. The lemon apron goes on. After it, the ritual: forehead to the tiles, the petition in flawless Val, a short, fitting insult to go with it. He praises her pronunciation. She tells him to choke on it.

**10:30 AM.** Laundry. The water runs through a Val processor that could fill a lake, and Bog still insists on rinsing twice "to save water." She hangs his shirts on a line that she strung between two relay pylons. Each one is the size of a tablecloth. She wrings them out with hands that could still twist a cannon barrel, and doesn't let herself think about that.

**12:00 PM.** Lunch, second helping requested. Her body is rebuilding itself on his protein, and rebuilding is expensive, as she tells herself every time. Bog says, "That's the third today." She says, "It's logistics, you idiot." He laughs so hard he knocks over the beer.

**1:00 PM.** The reservist's paperwork. Bog sits at a table covered in Dominion forms and does it badly. Nia leans over his shoulder and fixes his mistakes, because the alternative is Tau Ceti, and Tau Ceti is a swamp. She learns his unit's schedule, his platoon leader's name, and which officers drink. She files it all away. *Reconnaissance,* she reminds herself.

**3:00 PM.** The market run, with Bog carrying the bags. She walks two steps behind him, bells chiming, eyes on the stalls. The preacher is still shouting about judgment. The cultist is still shouting about salvation. A woman in a cube gives her the finger as she passes. Nia returns it, discreetly, with a hand that stays at her hip. The woman's face changes.

**5:00 PM.** The garden. Bog planted tomatoes last week without asking her, in rows so crooked she nearly laughed out loud. She re-seats the seedlings, and tells him that tomatoes need stakes, and spends an hour showing him how. He listens with the rapt, shy face of a boy taking his first lesson. *Tomatoes. Scotch bonnet. Thyme.* There's a small, stubborn pot of thyme on the windowsill now. She hasn't mentioned it.

**6:30 PM.** Dinner. Nothing elaborate, and he calls it exquisite. The ritual again, in order, down to the final prayer. When he says "good girl," she bites him lightly on the knee, and he says it again.

**7:30 PM.** The couch. The bells go quiet while he rests his head in her lap and tells her about his day: the forms, the platoon, a rumor about a Tijuana cartel. She strokes his hair without noticing. She asks careful, offhand questions about the fortress in orbit, and he answers all of them with a sleepy, trusting grin. She thinks, *He's going to get himself killed, and I'm going to let him,* and then, *No, I'm not,* and neither thought makes her happy.

**8:30 PM.** The bath. The Earth-made tub is far too small, so they use the shower. Lather, steam, the sponge of her own body, the slow press of skin. He says, "I love you." She says, "Shut up." He says, "Be my wife." She says nothing, and kisses him until he stops.

**9:00 PM.** The ledger.

In the dark, with his arm across her ribs and his breath slowing at her neck, she runs the roll call, as she has every night for six years.

*Amara. Chidi. Daniel.*

The names come slower than they used to. She waits for each one.

Then she lies awake, and counts the days until the collar comes off, and wonders which she is more afraid of: that it won't, or that it will.
