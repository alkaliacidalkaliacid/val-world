# THE ASHEN DOMINION
## Bog & Nia: Florence

---

## 1. The Clinic

"D-D-Dr. Hermes is dead."

The plump nurse, a timid woman who looked to be of Indian descent, wrung the hem of her apron as she said it.

"For real?" Bog asked.

Behind his head, the jetpack gave a wet cough, leaked a thin ribbon of smoke, and died with a long, sorry whine. He had flown the last forty kilometers on spite alone. The smell of burnt oil drifted through the lobby of the free clinic in Florence.

"H-H-He was a-attacked by a b-b-black bear," the nurse said. "L-Last month."

"Oh..."

The alien soldier stood very still in the silence, tall enough that the ceiling fan was turning just above his horns of hair. Somewhere under the smoke, his stun-numbed left arm gave a small, useless twitch.

---

## 2. The Bench

Bog flung his jetpack as far as his good arm would throw it, stretched out, and dropped onto an outdoor waiting bench with a groan. A nurse noticed the awkward way he cradled his left shoulder and came over with a case of Val medical equipment.

"Let me take a look at that."

"Much obliged."

She worked quietly, and after a while she asked in a low voice, "You're the volunteer who keeps bringing us medical supplies, aren't you?"

"Business, actually."

A brief silence followed.

"Even so, Dr. Hermes and the rest of us are truly grateful," she said. "You've saved so many lives. Especially the children."

"Well, that's good to hear."

The nurse let out a small, involuntary chuckle at his offhand reply.

"My name is Priya."

"Bog. Say, can I ask you something?"

"Of course. Go ahead."

"Has a super-beautiful, muscular Black girl come by here? Dressed like a bunny?"

(The "super-beautiful" was entirely Bog's own assessment, and a woman of Nia's age was hardly a "girl.")

"No, she hasn't."

"I see."

Silence settled over the bench. The treatment was finished.

---

## 3. Bhuta

Bog flexed his newly mobile arm, rolling the shoulder in a slow circle. "Impressive. Running Val medical equipment like that. You're quite the mechanic."

"Oh, no." Priya looked a little embarrassed. "I'm not really good with machinery. My husband had the knack for that. He used to help Dr. Hermes analyze the Val gear. My own specialty is superhuman medicine. It just so happens that some of it applies to Val as well."

In other words, she was less a nurse than a doctor, perhaps even a PhD. But Bog didn't give it much thought. He *was* Val, after all. He simply said:

"That's amazing."

He fell silent for a moment, then steered the conversation toward less intellectual ground.

"Are black bears dangerous?"

"Yes, especially since the war. There's even a rumor that they're possessed by *bhuta*."

"Whoa. That's wild. What's a *bhuta*?"

Priya swallowed hard and sat down beside him. "Malevolent spirits," she said. "The souls of people who died in the war and were never properly buried, so they linger on the earth."

She spoke with an intensity that seemed oddly out of character for a woman of such an intellectual profession.

---

## 4. The Heavy Air

Bog grimaced. "Ugh. A ghost possessing a savage beast? What am I supposed to do about that? Sure, I know the specters of those crystalline lifeforms from Alpha Centauri vanish if you douse them in the urine of an upside-down pill bug from the Barnard's Star system, but..."

Priya blinked at him. "Is that... a real thing?"

"Real thing. Very effective." He frowned. "Not very portable, though."

She laughed, quickly and softly, and then the laugh faded. She looked out past the clinic fence at the dark line of trees, her hands knotted in her lap.

"I've been able to see strange things since I was a child," she said. "Shapes that weren't there. People standing in rooms where nobody was standing. I could always tell when a place had something in it."

"Like a sixth sense?"

"Like a sixth sense that nobody else had." Her mouth twisted. "The other kids teased me, and then the adults did, and I started to wonder if I was simply losing my mind. Or if it was some kind of power. A superpower."

Bog said nothing, which was unusual for him. He seemed to be listening.

"So I started researching heroes. How they awakened, what they had in common, whether any of them saw things the way I do." She shook her head. "Then the war started, and I never finished. I still don't have an answer."

"Huh," said Bog. "Sounds like homework."

"It is, a little." She almost smiled, then stopped. Her voice dropped. "But I'll tell you something, whatever it is I have. Lately there's been a feeling over this area. A heaviness, like the air before a storm that never breaks. It started a few weeks ago, around the time Dr. Hermes died."

A cold breeze slid across the bench. Somewhere in the trees, something small and unseen went very quiet.

Bog's hand drifted, without his noticing, toward the place where his jetpack would have been.

---

## 5. An Invitation

Bog weighed his options. He could wait in Florence for Nia to turn up, or he could follow the chime of her bells and sweep the countryside as far as the old Alabama-Mississippi state line. In the end he decided to stay put for now, find a meal, a few drinks, and a bed, and let the rest wait until morning.

He had just pushed himself up from the bench when Priya spoke. If he didn't mind waiting until she finished her rounds, she said, he was welcome to a meal and a place to sleep at her home.

"Sure," said Bog, without a moment's thought.

Her house was one of Florence's dilapidated houses, patched and renovated into something livable. She shared it with her husband, their two teenage daughters, and her husband's mother. The husband greeted Bog warmly and thanked him for the medical supplies, and the daughters watched from the doorway with narrowed, wary eyes. The mother-in-law made her hostility perfectly clear, with a glare that could have stripped paint. Still, no one asked him to leave.

Bog was unbothered by any of it. He tucked into the Indian food with gusto, praised each dish in turn, and finished with a belch that rattled the cutlery. He didn't even miss the beer.

---

## 6. Dinner

The dining table was a door laid across two sawhorses, and every inch of it was covered. There was a pot of dal, a stack of rotis wrapped in a dish towel, rice heaped in a dented steel bowl, and an aloo gobi still hissing from the pan. A chicken curry the color of a sunset sat in the middle, with a jar of fiery pickle beside it that was clearly meant as a dare.

Bog sat at the head of the table because it was the only chair that could take his weight. His knees stuck out on both sides.

"Please," said Priya's husband, Arjun, a thin man with grease permanently ground into the creases of his knuckles. "Eat. Eat as much as you like."

"Don't mind if I do."

He did not mind. He tore a roti in half, scooped up curry with it, and swallowed the whole thing in one go. Across the table, the two teenage daughters watched him with the flat, narrowed look of girls who had been warned about men like this. The younger one, Kavya, held her fork like a weapon. The elder, Meera, had not touched her plate.

At the far end sat the mother-in-law, a tiny woman in a faded green sari. She glared at him over her spectacles like a customs officer who had found something in the luggage.

"Dadi," Priya murmured. "Please."

"I am eating," said the old woman. "I am allowed to look while I eat."

Bog took a spoonful of the pickle and chewed thoughtfully. His eyes watered. He nodded, deeply moved.

"This is good. This is *really* good. What is this?"

"Lime," said Arjun. "And green chili. My mother makes it."

"Your mother." Bog turned toward the end of the table. "Ma'am, I salute you. In my whole career I have never been this angry at a vegetable and enjoyed it this much."

Dadi's glare did not move. But her chin lifted a fraction of an inch.

"Do you have a mother?" Kavya asked suddenly. It sounded less like conversation than like an accusation.

"Kavya," Priya hissed.

"Nope," said Bog cheerfully, reaching for the rice. "Never did. We come out of a cradle. A big machine, lots of tubes, very efficient. Though the cradles are broken now." He frowned and added, as an afterthought, "Something got in them."

The table went quiet. Meera finally looked up. Arjun's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"No mother," Dadi repeated slowly.

"No, ma'am."

"No one to cook for you?"

"Well, there's the mess hall."

The old woman said something short and sharp in Hindi. Priya flinched. Arjun covered his mouth with his hand, and it was unclear whether he was hiding a laugh or a wince.

"What did she say?" Bog asked.

"She said," Priya began carefully, "that it explains a great deal."

"Ha!" Bog slapped the table so hard the dal jumped. "I like her."

"Don't," said Dadi.

He helped himself to more curry. Between mouthfuls he was an easy guest, praising the rice for its fluffiness and asking Arjun how a man with those hands had fixed Val medical equipment. Arjun answered with the shy pride of a craftsman who rarely got an audience. Bit by bit the room loosened. Kavya let her fork drop to the plate. Meera pushed a small bowl of cucumber raita an inch toward the giant without looking at him, which in her language seemed to be a treaty.

Bog tore off one last roti, wiped the pot clean, and sat back with his hands on his belly. Then he let out a belch that began in his boots and ended somewhere near the ceiling. A cup rolled off the table. Kavya burst into laughter before she could stop herself, and clapped a hand over her mouth, scandalized.

"Excellent," Bog sighed. "Ten out of ten. Only thing missing is a cold beer."

"There is no beer," said Dadi, with some satisfaction.

"I noticed."

He reached into the pickle jar one more time, then paused. He held a single glistening green chili between two thick fingers and studied it for a long moment. The thought was gone before he could name it. Something about a woman who liked her peppers hot, and a kitchen somewhere with a lemon-print apron on a hook.

He ate the chili himself.

Outside, the evening settled over Florence. Somewhere past the fence line a dog began to bark, then stopped abruptly, mid-note, as though a hand had closed around its throat.

Priya's eyes went to the window. So did Dadi's.

Neither of them said a word.

---

## 7. Night

In the middle of the night, a bear came into Florence and began to run wild.

The town had an elected sheriff who could be reached by radio, but it seemed unlikely he would arrive in time.

In the spare room, Bog was fast asleep and snoring like a sawmill. Val were known across the galaxy as a race of warriors, an epithet that suited him about as well as a tutu. Priya, on the other hand, woke instantly. She lay rigid in the dark for a heartbeat, feeling the wrongness in the air like a cold hand on the back of her neck. Then she shook Arjun awake.

Her husband grabbed the fire poker from beside the stove and helped her get the girls into their clothes and shoes, so the family could flee at a moment's notice. Dadi was already dressed and sitting upright on her bed, with her spectacles on and a face like stone.

Outside came the barking and then the screaming.

"That's the house next door," Arjun said, his voice cracking. "We have to help them!"

Priya ran to the spare room and shook Bog's shoulder with both hands.

"Ugh," he mumbled into the pillow. "Fighting ghosts isn't a soldier's job."

He said it as though he had ever done a proper soldier's job in his life. Then he rolled out of bed, glanced around the room, and picked up the nearest weapon he could find, which was a rolling pin.

"Well. I suppose I'll go take a look."

He stepped outside and locked eyes with the bear.

The animal stood in the middle of the street, in the light of a single burning porch, with a human being clamped in its jaws, head first. Bog was not an expert on the species. He did not know the average size of a black bear. But he was fairly sure it was not supposed to weigh two thousand pounds, and it was not supposed to stand taller than the neighbor's fence. And surely there was no reason for a shimmering purple aura to be pouring off its back like heat off summer asphalt.

"Right," Bog whispered.

He began to close the door. Slowly, politely, the way one withdraws from a conversation with a drunk.

The bear dropped the corpse. It threw back its head and roared, a sound that shook the dust out of the rafters, and then it charged. The door, the doorframe, and a good part of the wall disappeared in a burst of splinters. Bog, who was in front of them, went sailing backward into the house and hit the dining table, flattening it into the floor.

The rolling pin spun away across the boards.

---

## 8. Oppression

The bear tore through what was left of the doorway and into Priya's home, bringing a good part of the wall with it. For a moment it stood in the wreckage, nostrils flaring. It caught the scent of the sprawled, green, thoroughly unappetizing alien on the floor, and it snorted in open disgust.

Then it turned its head and made a beeline for Priya.

Arjun stepped into its path with the fire poker held in both shaking hands. He was a thin man with grease in the creases of his knuckles, and he planted his feet as though he meant to stay there. Behind him, the girls screamed. Dadi, in the doorway of the back room, said something in Hindi that was almost certainly a prayer.

"Hold on there, furball!" a voice called from the rubble.

Bog's head rose out of the splintered remains of the dining table, with a roti stuck to one horn of hair. He pointed at the bear with an accusing finger.

"Aren't *you* the one being invaded here? Where's your pride?" He heaved himself upright, shedding plaster and curry. "Don't worry. I'll show you how this is done. Time for some oppression!"

He lunged.

He did not so much run at the bear as fall toward it with an enormous green body, arms wide, and he caught the creature around its thick neck before it could swing. The impact shook the floor. Plates leaped off the shelves. The bear roared into his face, and its breath smelled of blood and old iron. It reared, raking at him with claws the length of kitchen knives.

"Ow! Hey! That's my good shoulder! Well, the other good one!"

Bog was a Val. He was seven and a half feet of bone and muscle, and in all his years of service he had never lost a wrestling match to anything that did not have a hull. He dug in his heels and squeezed.

The bear did not budge.

It was the aura that Priya noticed. It shimmered along the animal's back, and as Bog's arms tightened, the purple light began to pulse. It was slow and steady, like a heartbeat that was not the bear's.

"Bog," she said faintly. "It's not alone in there."

The bear's eyes rolled toward her. For one terrible instant they were not the eyes of an animal at all.

---

## 9. Cattle Mutilation

Val soldiers, even lazy ones like Bog, are massive and immensely strong. So are bears. Add a *bhuta*, a malevolent spirit with nothing to lose and no body of its own to protect, and it was hard to say who had the upper hand.

A green fist slammed into the bear's snout with a wet crack. The animal answered with a hairy forepaw that could have torn a human face clean off. Bog ducked, and the claws scored four furrows through the plaster where his head had been. The jaws snapped shut on empty air, then again, and then a third time so close that he felt the heat of its breath on his ear. He dropped his chin an instant before the teeth met. A skull-crushing bite closed by a hair's breadth above his scalp.

Another forepaw was already swinging at him.

Priya gasped.

"Priya!" Arjun shouted from across the room. "The Val medical laser! It's in my workshop! Go!"

She stared at him. Then she ran, bare feet slapping through the curry and broken crockery, and vanished down the back hallway. Dadi's voice rose behind her, still praying.

Arjun backed away from the doorway, putting distance between himself and his wife, and began beating the fire poker against a cast-iron pot. The clang filled the house.

"Hey! Hey, over here! Here, bear!"

The bear's head swung toward the noise. Its eyes narrowed on the thin, trembling man with the pot, who was clearly the better meal, and its weight began to shift.

Bog did not like that.

He was bleeding freely from the nose and wore a stripe of curry down one cheek. Both of these facts offended him. He gathered himself, drove upward off the floor with his knees, and slammed his skull into the underside of the creature's jaw. Teeth clacked together with a sound like a dropped crate of plates. The great head snapped back, and a spray of red went up across the ceiling.

"You do NOT," Bog roared, spitting blood, "ignore me when I'm about to hit you! I'm gonna subject you to a *cattle mutilation*!!!"

In the hallway, Priya skidded to a halt. She did not know what a Val cattle mutilation was. She was nearly certain she did not want to.

The bear shook off the blow. Along its spine, the purple aura flared bright, and the glow crept forward over its shoulders and up its neck, as though something were pulling itself out of the animal and into the light. Its mouth opened wider than any bear's mouth should.

What came out was not a roar. It was a voice, thin and many-layered, like a crowd of people speaking from the bottom of a well.

*Not... buried...*

Arjun dropped the pot.

Down the hall, a door banged open, and Priya's voice came back faintly over the noise. "Found it! I've got it, I've got the laser!"

Bog wiped his nose on his forearm and grinned. It was a terrible grin, all fangs and no sense.

"Great," he said. "Now somebody tell me how you shoot a ghost."

---

## 10. Test First, Verify Afterward

The bear gave Bog no chance to strike another pose. A single sweep of its forepaw slammed him flat against the floorboards, and the boards cracked under him. One heavy paw settled onto his chest and pressed down, as though it were stamping out a cigarette.

"Oof. Okay. That one's on me."

Priya came skidding back into the room with the laser in both arms. It was a squat, ugly thing, a hand-welded jumble of Val casing and hospital cabling, with a strip of masking tape across the side that read DO NOT USE in a neat engineer's hand. She did not stop to explain. She pushed it into her husband's hands and ran for the girls.

Arjun held a doctorate in engineering from Georgia Tech, where his specialty had been the design of advanced medical devices, and in the course of that education he had acquired one habit above all others. When confronted with the unknown, a man tested first and verified afterward. He did not stop to wonder whether the situation was a proper occasion for testing. He turned the output dial to its maximum, put the emitter against his shoulder, and sighted along the barrel, whose safety systems were still undergoing repairs.

"Hypothesis," he muttered, "is that this will work."

He fired.

The beam was a thin white line, and it passed through the bear's torso with a sound like tearing silk. A hole the width of a coin opened behind the shoulder. Purple light burst from the wound in a rush, like steam out of a ruptured pipe, and the aura came off the animal's back in thick, shrieking ribbons. The bear lurched forward with a bellow that was now clearly two voices, one of them a dying animal's, and it threw itself across the room at the man holding the weapon.

Arjun did not run. His knuckles went white on the grip.

"Second trial," he said.

The second shot took the animal through the chest. The third took out the knee. Paws that could have taken off a human face skidded across the floor, scattering plates and curry, and stopped a meter from Arjun's bare feet. The great body sagged and collapsed onto the ruins of the dining table, and then it lay still.

A last wisp of purple rose from the open wound and thinned into the air.

*...buried...*

It was gone.

Under the bear's paw, Bog drew a long breath.

"Does anybody," he wheezed, "want to get this thing off me?"

Arjun looked at the laser. The casing was too hot to hold, and a thread of smoke was curling up from the grip. He lowered it carefully to the floor.

"Results," he said, a little dazed, "were consistent with expectations."

Then he sat down hard in the middle of the broken room and started to shake.

---

## 11. A Charming Neighbor

Was it over? Far from it.

The wisp that had risen from the bear's wound had not vanished. It had simply gone looking for a softer place to live. Now it came drifting back across the room, low and slow, and it found a thin man sitting in the wreckage with his hands shaking in his lap.

A purple aura engulfed Arjun from head to foot. His shoulders went rigid. His head tipped back, and light poured out of his eyes in two hard beams that painted the ruined ceiling violet.

"Arjun?" Priya's voice came from the hallway, small and very far away.

He did not answer. He rose without using his hands, the way a marionette rises, and his head turned until the glowing eyes found the green alien pinned beneath the dead bear.

Then he lunged.

This was bad.

The bear's corpse still lay across Bog's chest, and the man was coming at him with his fingers hooked like claws and a sound in his throat that no engineer had ever made at Georgia Tech. Bog got one arm free and caught Arjun by the throat. The man's pulse hammered against his palm. His neck was thin and brittle, no thicker than a roti rolled up for the pan.

Bog could have snapped it. A flick of the wrist would have done it, and he would have felt nothing more than he felt cracking a cockroach leg at the black market. (That many other Val soldiers would have done exactly that without a second thought is beside the point. HE simply didn't want to.)

So he did not. He held the man at arm's length while those purple eyes burned inches from his face and the fingers clawed for his eyes. Arjun was far stronger than any thin man had a right to be. The arm holding him shook.

"Priya," Bog grunted. "Ugh. Do me a favor."

Behind him, the floor creaked. Priya had come back to the edge of the room, with both daughters pressed against her legs and her hand clamped over her own mouth.

"Explain to your husband," Bog said, through his teeth, "what a charming neighbor I am."

Arjun's mouth opened, and the many-layered voice came out of it, soft and terribly patient.

*Not... buried...*

Then it smiled, with a smile that was not his.

---

## 12. The Red Light

In the doorway of the back room, Dadi stood perfectly upright. Purple light streamed from behind her spectacles. She took one step, then another, with the stiff patience of a woman who had decided to do something and meant to see it done. Her hand shot out and closed around her granddaughter's ankle.

Kavya screamed. Meera screamed. The old woman said nothing, and her grip did not loosen as the girl was dragged across the floor.

"No way," Bog groaned. He was pinned under a dead bear with a possessed engineer's hands scrabbling at his face, and he was running out of arms.

Priya threw herself at her mother-in-law and tried to pry the brittle old fingers off the child. They were like iron bands. She pulled and sobbed and pulled again, and her hand brushed the wrinkled wrist, and a vision struck her like a bucket of ice water.

She saw the town of Florence from above, every street and roof laid out in the dark. A purple glow bloomed in one house, then another, then a dozen. Doors burst open. People in nightclothes poured into the road and fell on their own families and neighbors with bare hands. The light spread like fire across dry grass. It took the dogs, then the deer in the woods, then the crows on the power lines, and it rolled outward over the hills, over the river, over cities she had never seen, until the whole curve of the earth glowed violet and nothing moved on it. The killing went on until there was no one left to do it.

Then there was only the light, alone, and an enormous silence.

"NOOOOO!"

Red light burst out of Priya's body.

It was not a glow. It was a detonation, a shockwave the color of a forge, and it swept across the room in one roaring sheet. Dust and splinters flew outward. The purple haze was torn off Arjun and Dadi in shrieking rags and burned away in the air. Both of them dropped to the floor like cut strings, and Dadi's fingers finally let go. The windows rattled in their frames.

When the glare faded, Priya was standing in the middle of the room, and her sari was gone.

It had burned off her in strips, and what remained clung to her in scorched ribbons of cloth. Underneath was something else, a costume of deep crimson and black, formfitting and cut with a daring that no one, least of all Priya, had chosen. It hugged her curvaceous figure like a second skin. A band of gold gleamed at her waist, and a wide red sash streamed behind her in a wind that was not there.

Bog stared up from under the bear. His eyes went wide, and a slow, lecherous grin spread across his face.

"A hero," he breathed.

Priya looked down at him.

It was not the shy, stammering look of the nurse who had bandaged his shoulder. Her eyes had gone a clear, burning red, and they pinned him to the floor more firmly than the bear's corpse had. She was not looking at the alien's grin, or at his torn clothes, or at the bear. She seemed to be looking at something behind him and through him, the way she had looked at the dark tree line from the clinic bench.

Bog's grin faltered.

"What?" he said. "What is it? Do I have something on my face?"

She did not answer. Slowly, her head tilted, as though she were listening to a voice only she could hear.

Outside, beyond the shattered wall, the purple light was still rising over the rooftops of Florence.

---

## 13. The Red Mediatoress

The red light did not fade. It settled into the air of the ruined room like the glow of a banked furnace, and in the middle of it, Priya's lips moved.

"I am the Red Mediatoress."

The voice was hers, but it came from a long way below her own. It was calm, unhurried, and a little tired. She did not look at Bog when she spoke. Her burning eyes were fixed on a point somewhere inside her own chest, and the words seemed meant for the woman who lived there. In a sense they were. The nurse who stammered and the thing that spoke now were pieces of one person, and the second had waited a long time to speak to the first.

"Listen, Priya. There is not much time."

Bog, still pinned under the bear with Arjun's limp hand lying across his chin, opened his mouth to say something about that. He closed it again.

"The transcendent ones of this world fought the green ones who came from beyond," the voice said. "They used *siddhis* and *astras* to do it, and with every use, the fabric of the world grew thinner. Beneath this place, a singularity lay buried. It was sealed long ago and meant to stay sealed. The wars tore it open."

A pause. Out in the street, something howled, and it did not sound like a dog.

"Then came the fighting in the southern seas. More *siddhis*. More power poured into a wound that was already bleeding. That was the final blow. From the singularity, a *threshold* has begun to widen. The *bhutas* are merely what leaks through first. They are the least of it." Her mouth tightened. "After them, the cosmos itself will come apart."

"Southern seas," Bog murmured to himself. He frowned. For some reason he thought of a cold, wet burrow and a very long stretch of seabed, and then the thought slipped off him like water.

"So, are you a hero, then?" he asked aloud.

The Red Mediatoress did not so much as glance at him.

But Priya, inside, asked the same question. Her lips shaped it, trembling. *Are you a hero?*

This time the entity answered. It spoke slowly, as if each word were being weighed against something much heavier.

"I am neither *Dharmātman* nor *Pāpātman*. Neither *deva* nor *asura*. I am nearer to *Brahman* than to any of those." A silence, and a flicker passed across the burning eyes, rueful and almost shy. "No. That is not right. That word is too large, and it will only frighten you."

Priya's own tears ran down her face, and the voice continued in a gentler register.

"A cosmic being. That is closer. A being who happens to be wearing you."

Beneath the bear, Bog stared up at the crimson figure standing in the wreckage of a dining room, with a scorched sari at her feet and the end of the world in her mouth.

"Huh," he said. "That's a lot of paperwork."

---

## 14. A Compromise

The commotion in Florence ended the way a thunderstorm ends, all at once and with a great deal of damage left behind. The red light swept down the streets and the purple haze burned out of one person after another. Men and women dropped where they stood and woke up on the pavement with no idea why their hands were bloody. Somewhere in the dark, the last of the *bhutas* gave a long, thin sigh and was gone.

The Red Mediatoress stood in the ruins of the dining room and told Priya what came next.

"This is not the end of it. The *bhutas* are only a symptom. Unless the singularity is buried again, they will come back, and they will come back stronger." Her burning gaze turned westward, toward the wall that was no longer there. "It is my mission to find it. It lies somewhere in that direction."

Priya's hands twisted in the scorched ribbons of her costume. She looked at her sleeping husband, at her daughters huddled in the corner, and at the old woman lying in the wreckage with her spectacles knocked sideways. She was afraid. She was more afraid than she had ever been in her life.

But she had also spent thirty-odd years wondering why she could see what others could not, and now she knew. All those years of teasing and doubt, and the unfinished research, had been leading her here.

"All right," she whispered. "I'll do it."

"Good." The voice softened. "You must tell no one. Not your family, not your friends. What you carry is not meant to be known."

The burning eyes shifted, and they came to rest on the green alien still lying beneath the bear.

"He has heard everything."

"Hm?" said Bog.

The Red Mediatoress raised a hand, in the manner of a woman brushing a crumb off a table, and the air around Bog began to *thin*. The edges of his body went pale and transparent, and the floorboards under him were visible through his arm.

"Hey!" he yelped. "HEY! What are you, what is that, stop that!"

"Stop!" Priya screamed. She lunged and caught her own raised wrist with her other hand. "Stop it! Don't!"

The thinning halted.

"He is a witness," the entity said, without heat. "That is all. It is simpler to remove him."

"He brought us medicine! He brought the clinic medicine, he saved the children!" Priya was crying again. "Please. He's not an enemy. He's a good man."

"He isn't," Bog said weakly, from the floor. "Technically. But I appreciate the sentiment."

For a Cosmic Being, the lives of children in a small Alabama town were of no great significance. She knew it, and Priya knew it. But the voice that spoke through Priya's lips was also, after all, Priya's own, and this incarnation had made a request. It was not in the Red Mediatoress's nature to refuse the will of the one she wore.

The hand lowered. Bog's body slowly refilled, like a glass under a tap, and he fell back with a gasp.

"Reconsidering," said the Red Mediatoress, after a pause. "If he is to be preserved, he may as well be of use. Take the green one as a subordinate."

"A what?" said Bog.

"A subordinate," Priya repeated, uncertain. She looked at him. "Would you...?"

"I'm a sergeant," Bog said, with great dignity, and then, with less dignity, "Wait, which way are we going?"

"West."

"How far west?"

"To the old border," Priya said. "Between Mississippi and Alabama."

Bog's eyes slowly widened. For several seconds he was silent, which was not like him at all. It was the one line on the map he had planned to search anyway, and he was a Val soldier, with no real concept of fate. All he understood was that something had decided to be convenient, and he did not trust it.

"Fine," he said at last, "but you carry your own bags."

---

When Arjun woke, the sun was coming up through the hole in the wall and the house smelled of ash. He lay still for a moment, aware of a headache like a collapsed building, and of his wife kneeling beside him with her hand on his chest.

She told him as much as she could. It was not much. She told him she had to leave, that she was going west to the old border between Mississippi and Alabama, and that she could not explain why.

He sat up so fast the room swung.

"Then I'm going with you."

"Arjun..."

"You are not going alone. Not after last night. I won't hear it." His voice broke on the last word. He was gripping her hand hard enough to hurt.

"I'm not going alone." Priya looked across the room to where Bog was sitting on the splintered remains of the table, eating a cold roti off the floor. "He's coming with me."

Arjun stared. "Him?"

"Him," Bog confirmed, mouth full.

"He's very strong," Priya said, "and, in his own way, he's reliable." The roti was now more or less down to the crust. "I need you here. The girls need you. Someone has to hold the family together, and the clinic can't lose another one of us. Please. Stay."

He looked at her for a long time. He had been married to this woman for twenty years, and the person kneeling in front of him wore a crimson costume and her eyes still held the memory of fire. He did not understand any of it. But he knew when she had made up her mind.

"You come back," he said hoarsely.

"I'll come back."

"Priya."

"I promise."

He pulled her into his arms and held on. Behind them, in the corner, two girls watched their mother with their mouths open. At the other end of the room, Dadi had sat up, straightened her spectacles, and fixed Bog with the glare of a customs officer who had just been told the contraband was coming along on the trip.

"If she is hurt," said the old woman, in a voice like a cracked bell, "I will find you."

Bog swallowed the last of the roti.

"Yes, ma'am," he said, and, for once in his life, he meant it.

## 15. Dr. Hermes's Laboratory

"Hey, Arjun," Bog said, nudging a splinter of table aside with his boot. "Got anything I can hit things with? Something like that laser, only less likely to explode?"

Arjun, who was still sitting on the floor with his arm around his wife, looked up at him. His mind was working again, slowly. "The laser isn't finished. I only had the one." He thought for a moment. "If there's anything else, it would be in Dr. Hermes's laboratory. He kept all his projects there. Most of them I never understood."

"Where?"

"Behind the clinic. The shed with the green door."

"Thanks," said Bog, and he went out through the hole in the wall, stepping over the bear.

---

The laboratory was a converted tool shed, and it smelled of solder and old coffee. Bog stood in the doorway for a while with his hands on his hips.

Every surface was covered. There were jars of things that might have been organs, and coils of cable that might have been wiring or might have been intestines. A salvaged Val casing sat open on one shelf with its innards laid out in rows like a dissected frog. Next to it a thing like a brass beehive ticked quietly to itself. A blackboard was covered in equations and furious underlinings, with one sentence at the bottom ringed three times in chalk. Bog could not read it. The handwriting looked like a spider had been given a stroke.

He picked up a rod with a crystal on the end, shook it, and put it back. He opened a drawer full of what looked like very small, very sharp spoons and shut it again. A glass tube hummed when he held it near his ear, and he held it near his ear for a long moment, and decided he did not like what it said.

"Nope," he said to the room. "Nope. Nope. Huh. What's this? Nope."

He understood none of it. As a weapon store, it was a complete failure.

Then, on the workbench at the far end, he saw them.

Two gray alloy bands lay side by side on a folded cloth, and he recognized them at once. They were null collars. The inner surfaces were scratched where someone had tried again and again to get at the locking mechanism. Around them lay tools, and each was a labor of love. They were fine hooks and pry bars filed from spoon handles, a tiny saw made from a watch spring, and a set of tweezers bent into impossible angles. On the nearest collar the paint had been scraped off along one seam in a long, patient groove that went nowhere. Whoever had made it had worked at it for weeks.

Bog looked at the collars for some time. He turned one over in his hands, and the weight settled against his palm with the familiar, unwelcome feeling. It reminded him of something, though he could not quite say what.

Then he picked up both of them and clipped them to his belt with a clink.

"Nobody's using these," he said. It was not entirely clear to whom. "Might as well be useful."

---

When he came back to Priya's house, the family had gathered by the doorway. Priya had changed into plain clothes under the crimson, and the costume showed at her collar like a flame under a curtain. A canvas bag lay at her feet. Arjun was waiting with the laser, the DO NOT USE tape still stuck across its side, and he held it out to Bog with both hands.

"It overheats," he said. "Three shots and you have to let it rest, or it'll burn your hand. The safeties are... not good."

"I'll treat it with respect."

"Treat it with *fear*," Arjun said, with the severity of a man who had earned the right.

Bog took the laser and slung it over his shoulder. Priya hugged her daughters, one after the other, and held them for a long moment. She kissed her husband. Then she turned to the road, with the bag in her hand and the sun at her back, and the alien soldier fell into step beside her.

They went west.

## 16. Module Nine

Somewhere deep underground in Mississippi, though Bog may have forgotten all about it by now, the elite training of Sophia Ivanova as a second consort continued on schedule.

The burrow was warm and dim, lit by the slow amber pulse of the walls. In the middle of it, the tentacle monster went about its work with the unhurried precision of a clerk stamping forms.

"Module Nine," it announced, in its flat, courteous voice. "Core Control. Objective: voluntary regulation of the deep musculature, with the goal of improved performance and fertility readiness. Please begin."

"Пошёл ты," said Sophia ♥. "Пошёл ты, пошёл ты, пошёл ты ♥."

"Logged as 'greeting.'"

Sophia lay on a bed of soft cables, collared and belled, with her silver undercut plastered to her forehead. Her pale gray eyes were fixed on the ceiling with the grim focus of a soldier holding a position. The tentacles moved over and through her with patient, churning insistence, and they guided her as an instructor guides a student toward the correct form. Wherever the muscle clenched they pressed, and wherever it relaxed they waited, and it kept on that way.

"Hold," said the monster. "Release. Hold."

"I am not," she said between her teeth, "doing... your... exercise."

"Your muscular response is following the instruction at a rate of ninety-one percent."

"Это рефлекс ♥! Reflex! Involuntary!"

"Understood. Logged as 'reflex.' Please hold."

She held. Her jaw worked. A bell chimed, soft and pure, and then another, and the note rose and fell with the rhythm of her breathing, and she scowled at it as though it had insulted her mother. (It would have been a poor insult, given what everyone in the room knew about Val.)

"You are performing well, Sophia Ivanova," the monster said. "Control improved four percent over the previous session."

"Don't. Don't... praise me, you cable-brained..."

"Please define 'cable-brained.'"

"It means you have the... the intellect of a rope ♥!"

"Logged as a new entry. Vocabulary count: 335."

Sophia's eyes flicked toward the corner of the chamber, where the holographic scoreboard hung in the air in glowing amber digits. NIA OKONKWO sat at the top. SOPHIA IVANOVA sat below, with a gap of nine beside her name that had not changed in two days.

She stared at it. The tentacles tightened, very slightly, as though they had noticed.

"Current standing," the monster said gently, "remains second."

"Я знаю."

"You may improve it. The Commander has noted that quality of service is evaluated on three axes. Control is among them."

Her jaw went tight.

"Commander is a *cucumber*," she said. Her voice was hoarse, and the ♥ sat in the middle of it like a stone in a shoe. "A great big, green, overgrown, magnificent... *cucumber*..."

"Logged as praise."

"НЕТ."

"Logged as emphasis."

The tentacles shifted pace, and Sophia's breath snagged. For a moment her careful features dissolved, and the laconic assassin who had once vanished into portals in the middle of a firefight was a flushed woman trying not to laugh and not to cry. Her heels dug into the cables. Her whole body tightened and then, to her fury, let go on cue, just as the monster had asked, in a long unbroken wave.

A silence followed.

"Excellent," said the monster. "That was a personal best."

She lay there, chest heaving, arms flung wide.

"...cough," she said at last.

"Logged as cough."

"It was a *cough*. And it was a tactical analysis of... ♥ ...structural load."

"Understood."

Somewhere far to the north, in Alabama, a man with a stolen laser on his shoulder sneezed explosively in the middle of a road, and a woman in a red-trimmed costume glanced at him with some concern.

In the burrow, the amber lights dimmed to rest. Sophia stared up at the ceiling with her pale eyes half-closed and her lips moving silently on a phrase she'd said a hundred times. It came more slowly now, and she was less sure of it each time she said it. *Не сдамся. Не сдамся. Не...*

"Module Nine is complete," the monster said. "Rest period: ninety minutes. Module Ten will address sustained endurance. Please prepare."

"...Please define," said Sophia, very quietly, "*prepare.*"

Neither of them was sure whether she was joking.
