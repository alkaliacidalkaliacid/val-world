## Chapter 53: The Bunker


The wire was exactly where he expected it to be.

Darkwing crouched at the base of a pine and ran two gloved fingers through the needles, the way another man might check a pulse. He stopped when he found it, a hair-thin line of copper buried an inch deep and running off through the roots. He drew it up, clipped it to a device the size of a cigarette case, and flipped a switch without ceremony.

Half a mile away, a flare screamed into the sky and burst orange over the canopy. It was a tracer, one clean streak, aimed nowhere near them. The buzzing in the south swung toward it like a compass needle. The beating of wings rose into a frantic whine, then dwindled, then thinned into nothing.

"Scout drone," Darkwing said. "Two minutes of patience, and it would've had our heat signature. Rin, lights."

"Yes, Boss."

The suit's lamps came back up. Nia lay in the suit's palm, looking up at the man in the cowl, and felt something she had not felt in six years.

It wasn't hope. Hope was too big a word. It was recognition. He had planted a wire in a forest, in the dark, on the *chance* a drone might come, and when it came he had been two steps ahead of it without breaking his stride. This was not a man who had survived. This was a man who had *continued*. After the Meridian Line and the collapse and six years of ash, he was still doing precisely what he had always done, which was refusing to be a victim of the situation.

*One of ours,* she thought. *He's one of ours.*

The thought was so warm that she nearly missed the faint, absurd flutter of her own bells as the suit stepped over a root.

---

The hideout was a U.S. Army storage bunker, a concrete throat cut into the side of a hill, with a rust-eaten blast door that Rin opened with a pulley and a lot of enthusiasm. Inside, someone had hung bedsheets from the pipes and strung bulbs from a car battery. There were bunks and cook-fires and a wall map pinned with a hundred different colors of thread. There was soup. Nia could smell it from the door, and her stomach made its small treacherous sound again.

They put her on a cot in a side room, and four women came to look after her.

None of them were professionals. She could tell at a glance: one had been a dental hygienist, one taught second grade, one was a retired postal worker with a rosary around her wrist, and the youngest, barely nineteen, was learning on the job and trying to hide how much she shook. They cut the cuffs off with bolt cutters, cleaned the scrapes on her knees and elbows, and checked her for fractures with great gentleness. When they got to the collar, they looked at it, looked at each other, and left it alone. The nurse who had been a hygienist whispered something about "doctors at the Clinic."

The piercings were a different matter. They tried pliers, a jeweler's saw, and a small, hopeful application of vinegar. The rings did not so much as scratch.

"I'm sorry," the schoolteacher said. "We just can't. Not without cutting you."

"Leave them," Nia said. She sounded calmer than she felt. "They're Val alloy. They'll outlast us all."

The nurses nodded, relieved, and started gathering their instruments. Nia watched them, lying very still, and spoke before she could change her mind.

"One more thing. The beads."

They stopped.

She had hoped one of them would simply understand. Nobody did. The schoolteacher stood with a roll of gauze in her hand, her face doing something complicated. "The..."

"The ones you took out." Nia stared at the ceiling. She had decided, somewhere on the road, that the right tone was *clinical*. "Put them back."

"Back *where?*" the youngest whispered.

"Where they belong."

The silence in the room was the kind that has a weight. The postal worker crossed herself. The schoolteacher opened her mouth, closed it, and finally said, in the brave voice of a woman who had survived six years by not asking questions, "Of course. Whatever you need."

They did it quickly, with eyes averted, and without a word of comment. Nia let them, her jaw set, her gaze fixed on a water stain above the bunk. She offered no explanation. The honest explanation was that the devices processed her waste, that she had not eaten ordinary food in a very long time, and that a body can be trained into a dependency faster than it can be retrained out of one. The dishonest explanation, which was the one she told herself, was *tactical maintenance of cover.*

*Sanitation,* she thought grimly. *It's sanitation.*

They found her a pair of loose overalls, soft with age and two sizes too big, and helped her into them. The cloth was clean and smelled of lye soap. For a few seconds she sat on the edge of the bunk with her face half-turned, one hand flat against the faded denim, before she noticed the youngest nurse watching her with an expression that was equal parts pity and awe.

"You're really her," the girl whispered. "My brother had your poster."

"Tell him I said sorry about the haircut," Nia said.

---

Darkwing came in after they had gone. He did not sit. He stood at the foot of the cot with his cowl pushed back, revealing the weathered face of a man in his sixties with a tight gray beard and eyes that had not stopped moving.

"The rings," Nia said, before he could speak. "They reveal my location. Val alloy. They'll have a location on me within the hour."

"They did," he said. "I've taken care of that."

"How?"

"I have an excellent engineer." He just said so.

Nia stared at him. Then, for the first time in a very long time, she laughed. It was a short, cracked sound, but it was real.

He let her finish. When she had, he said, without any change of tone, "Vesper is gone. The Manor, the Cave, Manfred. The children I trained. I lost all of it in the first month." He said each item like an entry in a ledger. "I've been rebuilding what I can. Rin is the first one who stayed."

"She's good," Nia said.

"She's *loud,*" said Darkwing, and something moved at the corner of his mouth that might, in a different decade, have become a smile. "She's also very good."

Through the wall came the faint sound of a girl singing a cartoon theme in two different keys. Nia closed her eyes and listened.

*Humanity isn't lying down,* she thought. *It isn't. Not all of it.*

When she opened them, Darkwing had set something on the foot of her cot, and he was looking at her very steadily.

It was a gray case, scuffed at the corners, with a stenciled mark on the lid that read **CLASS 5**.

"I think," said Darkwing quietly, "you'd better tell me where you got this."
