## Chapter 94: Resolution

Priya crouched at the lip of the seam and peered down into it the way she would have peered into a well, one hand flat on the warm rock, her whole body doing the arithmetic of the drop.

"It goes d-down a long way," she said. "We will need to go s-sideways first, I think, where the crack is wider, and then— and then—"

The word stopped.

It did not trail off. It ceased, the way a lamp ceases — and in the silence after it, Nia felt the hair lift on her arms.

Heat came off Priya's skin. Not the heat of the sun-baked mountainside; this rose out of her, a flush that started in her face and ran down her throat and her arms like something being poured in under the skin, red as a coal fanned open. The air above her shoulders began to shiver. Priya straightened up out of her crouch slowly, unhurriedly, and the movement was wrong — too even, too considered, nothing in it of the woman who had sat down hard on the scree a minute before.

"Priya," Nia said.

The sari charred at its edges. It did not catch and burn; it flaked, curling away in black scales that dissolved before they reached the ground, and underneath it there was no skin and no nurse's uniform but a costume — crimson and black, close as a second hide, a band of gold at the waist. A wide red sash lifted off her shoulder and streamed out behind her, full and rippling, in a wind that touched nothing else on the mountain. It did not stir Nia's hair. It did not stir the dust.

Priya's eyes opened red.

Nia knew that red. She had stood on this mountain and heard what spoke out of this woman's mouth, heard it dispose of Bog like a piece on a board. The wanting in her own body, which had been a drumbeat all afternoon, went very quiet.

"Dharmatman," the Red Mediatoress said.

Her voice was Priya's voice with everything human taken out of it, the way a bell is a voice with the throat taken out of it.

"Rsi. You carry both names, little hero. Attend to what is laid on them."

"I don't—" Nia said. "What—"

"A new astra has been loosed upon the world." The red eyes did not blink. "Iron-wrapped. New-made, in the manner of your kind's latter days. It was spent against the dead, by the one who stands beneath your hand. The account of it is yours. Render it."

Nia stared at her. Beneath her hand. An astra — she had the word only as a shape, a thing out of the old epics her grandmother's generation still quoted, a weapon of gods. Nothing in her life fitted it. Nothing except — a mile of violet sparks going up into the sky, and a thirty-foot suit kneeling dark and dead on a ledge, and Rin laughing with shock inside a collar an hour later. Rin. Rin was beneath her hand — she was the escort, she had been charged with the girl, she had led her up this mountain.

She had no idea what account she was supposed to render, and the thing wearing Priya looked at her the way weather looks at a roof.

"Ignorance," the Mediatoress said, after a moment, "is not an account." She said it without anger, the way one notes a measurement. "See that it is not offered twice."

Then her face turned — a fraction, away from Nia, toward nothing on the mountainside — and what came next came colder, and slower, and Nia understood with a drop in her stomach that she might not be the one being spoken to at all.

"Your kind widens the tears and calls it survival. It tears the fabric wider with every generation and begs the fabric to hold. There is no saving it. It is the calamity it fears." The red gaze rested on the middle distance, on something behind the world. "When the burying is finished, and the threshold is shut — a resolution will be necessary."

Her lips moved once more, soundlessly, as if she were listening to an answer given from inside her own skull. Whether the words had been meant for Nia, or for the woman whose mouth had spoken them, or for no one, Nia could not tell, and somehow that was the worst of it — worse than the reproach. The reproach had at least wanted something from her.

The Mediatoress rose.

She went up off the rock without effort or gesture, the sash streaming above her now like a banner in a gale no one else could feel, and she hung for one moment over the black seam with the cold of it breathing up around her — and dropped.

Straight down. No climb, no descent of hands and footholds, no sideways scramble where the crack ran wider. She fell into the earth the way a stone falls into a well, red light dwindling, and the dark took her, and the seam breathed its cold up into the daylight as if nothing at all had passed.

Nia stood alone on the mountainside.

Below her, somewhere in that dark, were Bog and Rin. And now Priya — except that it was not Priya going down to them, and it did not intend to ask anyone's leave about anything it did when it got there. The plan the two women had made, the careful sideways descent, the watching over her — all of it was finished, and it had taken less time than the laugh that had ended the warning.

"Right," Nia said, to nobody. Her voice sounded very small and very ordinary. "Alone, then."

She got down on her knees at the lip of the seam, swung her legs into the cold, and began to climb down into the dark.
