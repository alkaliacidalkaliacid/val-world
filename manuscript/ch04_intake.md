## Chapter 4: Intake


Bog's home in the Wasteland was a squat, repurposed relay station on the edge of a dead freeway, and he'd barely shouldered through the door before he propped the Huntress against the wall and aimed his wrist-projector at her. The holo-photo flickered, froze, and fed into the reserve application. He submitted it before he could change his mind.

The reply came while he was still pulling off his boots. *Provisionally accepted.* Bog let out a breath that went on a bit too long. The Tau Ceti posting had dissolved from his schedule - provisionally, like the acceptance itself, but dissolved all the same - and with it the vision of a planet that was all bog, which struck him as a terrible joke.

"All right," he said, turning to her. "Intake."

She stared at him with an expression that could have cracked concrete.

"Kneel. Sit back on your heels. Hands behind your head, and look at me."

For a moment he thought she'd refuse. Her jaw was tight enough that the muscle bunched under her scar, and the collar flickered at her throat as if waiting for an excuse. Then she complied, slowly, with her teeth gritted the whole way down. Maybe the merchant hadn't lied about the training. The bells at her chest and hips gave one clear, sweet chime as she settled, and the sound, so lovely and so wrong, seemed to make her flinch more than the collar ever had.

Bog cleared his throat and tried to look like he knew what he was doing. He had read the registrar's manual twice. It had not covered this.

"Profile," he said. "'Black Huntress' can't be all of it. Names, history, everything. Your pack, where you were raised, what you've done. I'm your owner, so I have a right to know."

She growled. It was a low sound, almost a purr in its menace. Then she began to talk.

"My name is Nia Okonkwo." Her voice was flat and rough, an instrument that had been used as a weapon for years. "I was born in Maryland, raised in a three-room apartment above a laundromat, and I woke up one night when the building next door was on fire and there was a kid in a window. I didn't think. I just went. That's the whole origin story, you overgrown pickle."

"Pickle," Bog repeated, and filed it away.

"I was a physical therapist's assistant. I taught old men to walk again after their strokes. I was better at that than I was at hitting things, and I was *very* good at hitting things." Her eyes didn't move from his. "I held the Baltimore corridor for nine days. Thirty thousand people walked out through it. You can put that in your little form."

Bog entered it, laboriously, on the projector.

"My pack," she went on, and the word dripped with contempt, "is none of your damn business. They're alive. They're somewhere you'll never find them. And if you so much as look in their direction, I'll rip your spine out of your back and use it to hang the curtains."

"That's not very helpful for the form."

"Good."

He watched her for a moment. She was shaking, though not from fear. She held herself like a woman bracing against a flood. Somewhere in the pit of his gut, something shifted uncomfortably.

"Right," Bog said at last, and turned off the projector. "Well. That's... a start."

She spat a word at him in a language he didn't recognize. Whatever it meant, the way it landed made the bells hum.

"How many?" Bog asked. He kept his eyes on the form. "At the front. How many of us did you, ah, comfort?"

Her jaw worked. The silence stretched until the bells at her hips trembled with the effort of her holding still.

"Four hundred and twelve," Nia said at last. She said it the way a medic reads out a casualty count, flat and exact, each word bitten off. "I counted every one. I'll keep counting until I can start settling the account."

Bog blinked. He did the arithmetic twice, and it still didn't come out right.

"Four hundred and twelve," he repeated, "and not one of them took?" He looked up, honestly baffled. "The Elites would pay a fortune for that kind of record. The cradles have been dying for years, and you're telling me you walked through half a regiment and came out empty?"

"You think a womb is a vending machine, you green idiot?" Her lip curled back from her teeth. "My people have a saying. The spirits only open the door for someone worthy. They watched every one of you come and go, and not one of you got past the threshold." Her eyes found his, and they were very steady. "That's the real reason the Elites want me, isn't it? They're desperate, and the door won't open for them. And you won't be the one it opens for either. I promise you that."

Bog opened his mouth to argue, found nothing in it, and closed it again.

"And since you're so interested in my record," Nia went on, her voice dropping into something almost conversational, "let me tell you what happens when this collar comes off. I start with the knees. I take them one at a time, slowly, so you have time to appreciate each one. Then the elbows. By the time I get to the neck, you'll be asking me to hurry." She tilted her head. "And then I'll walk out of here and find the next one. And the next. Four hundred and twelve, Bog. I've got a lot of work ahead of me."

The bells chimed softly as she settled back on her heels, hands still locked behind her head, glaring at him with an unbowed patience that made the hair on his arms rise.

Bog put down the projector and went to find a drink. Whatever the Elites had been warning him about, it wasn't this.
