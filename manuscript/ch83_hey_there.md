## Chapter 83: Hey There

There was nothing to be done about the suit.

It had given everything it had to one last, glorious, orange wave, and what it had left was a dead dashboard, a cold engine, and a gravity well. Rin pulled every lever she could reach. She stamped on the pedals. She begged. And the nine-meter machine, with its school-bus-less shoulder and its mismatched plating and its cat sticker, tipped over the edge of the world and fell.

"*Waaahhh!!!?*"

She fell for a long time. She hit a ledge, bounced, hit a spur of rock, bounced again, scraped a wall with a scream of metal that sent sparks across the dark, caromed off something that gave a very loud *bong*, and kept on falling, tumbling, arms and legs of the suit flailing like an upturned beetle. The purple aura, which had been trying to eat her, was rushing up past the cockpit glass in thick, glowing, boiling streams. It was somewhat like being dropped down a chimney. It was somewhat like being *held*. It buoyed her, slowed her, took a little of the sting out of each bounce, and when she finally struck the bottom, it was with a long, shuddering, groaning *crump*, like a tin can landing on a feather bed.

Silence.

Rin lay on her back in the cockpit with her goggles knocked over one eye and her mouth tasting of copper.

"...Ow," she said.

She took inventory. Head: attached. Arms: attached. Legs: also attached, and complaining. Her whole body ached, from the roots of her teeth to the soles of her feet, a deep, bruised ache like she'd been played as a drum, but nothing seemed broken. She was alive. She was, most surprisingly, intact.

Because of the cockpit.

"Make the cockpit the sturdiest part," she recited, in a deep, gravelly, gloomy voice that was most of the way to a very good impression of a certain billionaire in a cowl, "and let the surrounding structure be more fragile. That way the outer shell breaks away to dissipate the impact, and the cockpit stays safe." She stared up at the cracked glass. Then, in her own voice, small and wobbly: "Thanks, Boss."

Nobody answered.

She looked around.

The place she had landed was a vast hollow in the rock, a bottomless cavern lit from nowhere, with a faint hazy violet glow that seemed to seep out of the very stone. The ceiling was lost in dark. The walls were slick and wet and curved like the inside of a throat. She could hear water dripping somewhere far off. She could hear nothing else at all. There was no sign of an exit, no rope, no ladder, not a single scrap of daylight, and the air was thick and bleak and oppressive, as if someone had put a lid on the world.

She tried the suit again.

Levers. Switches. The little red button she had never been allowed to press. A frantic ten minutes with a wrench through the hatch of the engine bay, muttering to herself, talking to the machine as to an old friend. *Come on. Come on, girl. Don't do this to me. I know you're tired. I'm tired too.* But the great machine lay on its back with its arms folded across its chest, as stubborn and still as a stone saint, and nothing she did would make it move.

At last she gave up.

She crawled back into the cockpit, pulled the hatch down over her head, drew her knees up to her chest, and sat there in the half-dark with her arms wrapped round her shins. She did not cry. Rin did not cry. She had decided, a long time ago, when she was fourteen and the sky was on fire, that crying took too much of the air.

But it was *quiet*.

That was the trouble. She had not noticed how loud the world had been, until it stopped. Normally the boss would have swooped down by now, cape snapping, one gauntleted hand outstretched, to haul her up by the scruff and growl, *Don't slack off.* Or Nia, grumpy, sharp-tongued Nia, whom she had somehow, already, in the way of the very young, grown to adore, would have shown up, scolding, wanting to know *what on earth she thought she was doing.* Somebody would have come.

The silence went on for a long time.

"Hey there!"

Rin's head snapped up.

The voice was far away and far above, bright, easy, completely unbothered, and it echoed down the shaft like a stone dropped in a well. Rin slammed her palm against the hatch, forced it open an inch, then wider, and craned her neck.

Something was coming down the wall.

It was not falling. It was *bounding*, in long, loose, springy hops from one rocky outcropping to the next, with the jerky, many-limbed nimbleness of an insect crossing a ceiling. It came out of the dark in a flapping coat, tumbling and kicking and pushing off the purple streams as they rose, like a swimmer pulling himself through the sea, and as it neared she saw the big green hand waving and the broad, shameless, delighted grin.

It was Lucky Val!

"Haha, this is actually kind of fun!" He sailed past a spur of rock, caught it with one hand, spun, and dropped again, arms spread wide, hair whipping, coat flying out behind him like a cape. "Isn't it? This stuff blowing up from below, it makes you feel weightless! Like being a feather! Want to join me?"

Rin stared at him.

She was a hero, in a manner of speaking. He was a Val. He had a face like a block of green marble and shoulders like a doorframe, and there was probably a very good and well-reasoned rule somewhere about not being delighted to see him.

"...Yes," said Rin, in a very small voice, and the word came out wet and shaky and ashamed. "*Yes.* Please. Yes."
