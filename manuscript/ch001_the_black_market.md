## Chapter 1: The Black Market


Bog ambled through the black market with his thumbs hooked in his belt, the way a man walks through his own backyard.

The market had taken over the bones of an old multistory car park. Bog ducked under the lowest of the cables without thinking about it; the place, like everything else on Earth, had been built for people a good two feet shorter than he was. Cables ran down from the ceiling in loose, sagging bundles, tapped from some Dominion relay that nobody would admit to knowing about, and the stolen current made the strings of bulbs overhead flicker and hum. Under them, the ash-gray light of the afternoon barely reached the stalls. The air was thick with frying oil, scorched metal, and the sweet chemical reek of something being distilled in a bucket. Boots and bare feet had worn a track through the grit, and a thousand voices haggled over it in six languages.

Everything was for sale. Crates of ration bars still stamped with Dominion glyphs sat beside piles of salvaged phones that would never ring again. A boy sold "genuine Val armor plating," which was clearly a hubcap. Real Dominion supplies were stacked behind a tarp, the kind the Polished said must never pass into human hands. Bog gave them a long look and a shrug. The Polished said a lot of things. Besides, the profit was good, the thrill was better, and nobody had ever gotten a medal for being a boring soldier.

There were drug tables where the vendors didn't even bother to look at him, because nothing they sold had any effect on a Val. There was illicit booze, which most certainly did. Bog had learned that the hard way and still remembered the morning after. In the back, in cages draped with oilcloth, something clicked and chittered. It might have come from another world, or from a supervillain's basement. Either way, the Polished had forbidden it, which of course was why it drew the biggest crowd.

Then there was the stretch of the market he usually avoided. The women were kept there in a row of metal lattice cubes, each of them crouched with her back to the aisle, so that what the row mostly presented to a passing buyer was a long, neat line of backsides. And they were, by an absurd margin, the healthiest things for sale in the city. Everyone knew why. The Elites protected them, so they were fed and watered better than half the garrison. It showed - and the women knew it, too, which was the real problem. A Val was not allowed to do them any serious harm, and every one of them was perfectly aware of it. So each of them poked both hands out through the lattice beside her hips and raised a middle finger at every buyer who passed. The first one did it. Then the next, then the whole row, a ripple of defiance running down the line of arses. Bog grunted. They were the most ill-tempered merchandise in the market, and he only wanted to walk through that row on days when he felt ready to wrestle a superhero and win. Today was not that day.

One of them nearly changed his mind anyway. Near the end of the row was a big black woman, broad in the shoulder and broader in the hip, with a metal collar at her throat - the only collar in the whole row - and a broad, bare backside that stopped him mid-step. He had seen those curves before. On a battlefield, of all places, in the middle of a war he was supposed to be fighting, and he had taken a full second out of the fighting to admire it, on the grounds that the finest arse on the planet deserved at least one witness from the invading army. It was still the finest arse he had ever seen, and the woman carrying it looked sturdy enough to survive him. Bog considered the oath, and the registrar, and the snug little Earth house he kept promising himself, and the furniture she would get to watch him break. Then he walked on.

He slipped into the crowd instead. On his left, a man in a filthy coat was shouting at the ceiling, certain that judgment was coming for the invaders and that every tooth in every green head would be counted. On his right, a woman in white robes was singing about the Val as humanity's saviors, her voice rising to drown him out. They stood a few feet apart and glared at the sky, not at each other. Bog passed between them like a man between two radios on different stations.

Earth had grown on him. He had stopped missing the homeworld somewhere around year three, and now he was thinking, seriously, about a house. A proper Earth house, white picket fence and all — cozy, solid, somewhere to settle down for good. His comrades had started buying slaves, mostly for chores, and eventually for the other thing the Elites cared about, the thing that no Val had ever managed on his own. He'd have to swear the oath, of course, before the registrar and the glowing seal, to keep the human healthy and well. The Elites were strict about that. Bog had a heavy hand and a talent for breaking furniture, so he would have to choose someone sturdy.

But that was a decision for another day.

A skewer stand steamed at the end of the aisle. The old man behind it was turning something long and dark and glossy over the coals, and as Bog leaned closer, a leg curled in the heat.

A giant cockroach.

"Ew," Bog said.

He pulled out a coin and held it out anyway.
