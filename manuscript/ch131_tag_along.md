## Chapter 131: Tag Along

The second helping had gone the way of the first: flawless. Sonya said the thanksgiving with her arms pressed to her sides and every syllable placed, and then, still kneeling in the dew-cold broomsedge with the prayer barely finished, she swayed once and folded sideways into sleep — out completely, mid-pride, the bells at her throat gone quiet against her skin.

Bog sat with her a moment. Then he reached over and patted her head, a slow, soothing hand over her hair, and she did not stir at all.

He picked her up — a tall woman's worth of deadweight sleep, limbs everywhere — and carried her to the monster, where a compartment opened in its flank. He settled her inside the way the monster had taught him she liked to be settled, tucked her arms in, and watched the flesh close over her.

Then he turned for the house.

"Commander Bog," the monster said. "A request."

A piece of it was coming away as it spoke: a small cluster of tentacles, no bigger than a farm cat, detaching itself and dropping into the broomsedge beside him. Around the base of the cluster sat a collar — depot stock, one of the ones the monster had absorbed up in orbit, out of the same inventory as the collar it had put on Sonya. The little cluster tried to arrange itself upright, tipped, and righted itself with great concentration.

"This portion asks to accompany you."

Bog looked at it. "Whoa there," he said. "It looks a bit clumsy."

"It is newly detached, Commander Bog. It will learn."

"Fine," Bog said. "You can tag along. But make sure the humans don't spot you. Any of them. Especially the grandmother."

The path back ran through the oak-and-hickory woods, red earth soft underfoot, and Bog moved the way he had been taught to move: quiet as a held breath, a soldier of the Val. The little monster kept pace somewhere off to his left, a faint dry rustling in the leaf litter.

His toe caught a limestone pebble jutting out of the red earth — a pale little knuckle of stone he had stepped clean over on the way in without ever seeing it. The pebble did not roll. It went like a bullet, cracked off a sweetgum trunk somewhere ahead in the dark, and was gone.

Bog stood on one foot, holding the other, listening to the whole woods listen back.

"Holy Val," he said, under his breath.

He went on more carefully after that, which was how he walked into the mountain laurel. A low branch caught him across the chest; he put a hand up too late, and the branch snapped with a crack like a green stick over a knee — and the laurel gave up its warblers. A dozen sleepy birds burst out all at once over his head, scolding fit to wake the county, and kept scolding as they scattered through the dark.

The little monster said nothing. Nothing at all.

Bog cleared his throat. "Path's changed since I came through," he said, to no one, and walked on with great dignity, the warblers still at it above him.

The house was dark when he reached it. He let himself in quietly and went down the hall on the stealth that had served him so well, and at Nia's door he stopped and peeked in. She was asleep at last — on her side, the bell at her throat still, one hand curled up near her face, her breathing slow and even. He looked for a moment. Then he eased the door to and went to his own room.

The bed was a cramped iron one, and it received him the way it always did: he sat, and the frame creaked; he lay back, and the frame let out a long, mournful creak and settled under him like an old dog. Outside the window the sky had gone grey at the edges, and the first light of dawn was brightening the room by degrees — the pine dresser first, then the chair with his uniform folded over its back, then the ceiling.

Across the ceiling, unhurried, upside down and entirely at home, the little monster was crawling.
