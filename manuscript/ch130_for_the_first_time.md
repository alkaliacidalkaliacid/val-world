## Chapter 130: For the First Time

He finished in her the way the tide finishes — all at once, and then everywhere.

She felt it go into her in pulses, deep where he had been striking, filling the place he had spent the night teaching her to open; and her body, the traitor instrument, received it with a long shudder that was not hers to command. Bog held her through it with both arms, his forehead against hers, and when the last of it had eased out of him he did not let her go. He kissed her — slowly, thoroughly, savouring it the way a man savours the last of a meal he has waited for — while their breath went up white together into the cold.

Then he stood, and took her with him.

He lifted her clean off the ground with her legs still around his waist, rose to his full height with the assassin in his arms like a child being carried from a bath, and only then — gently, by degrees — eased her down his body until his member, still erect, only slightly softened from its full monstrous rigidity, slid free of her with a wet, reluctant sound. What he had left in her stayed in her. She felt it, heavy and warm, as he lowered her the rest of the way and laid her down on the grass of the clearing, on her back, in the monster's glow.

He knelt beside her. His great hand came down and stroked her hair — the ruined, sweat-damp mane of it — back from her face, once, and again, unhurried, the way you stroke a cat that has come in out of the rain.

Sonya lay still and breathed. The grass was cold through the sweat on her back. The bells had finally stopped, and in the quiet she could hear the woods again, and her own heart coming down out of her throat by stages, and Bog's breathing beside her, already even, already content, a man for whom the world had recently been exactly as it should be.

"Are you hungry?" he asked.

She turned her head and looked up at him, and the irritation flashed across her face like lightning across a familiar landscape — because of course. Because her stomach, at that exact moment, chose to announce itself, audibly, into the clearing: the emptiness in her that the regimen had built and the night's work had deepened, the hunger that was not for food. Because he knew. Because he was *asking*, politely, as if it were a question, as if she were a guest at his table and not —

She nodded.

Once. Sharply. Like a woman signing something under duress.

Bog smiled, and sat back on the tree stump, and beckoned — one finger, curling, in the monster's light.

And Sonya Ivanova rose from the grass, and folded herself down.

The bells went first, as they always do — a small, bright scattering of notes as she prostrated herself on the cold grass of the clearing, forehead to the earth, back bared to the moon, in the posture she had never in her life taken before any living thing. It should have been clumsy. It should have been the halting, humiliating first attempt of a novice, and some part of her was braced for Bog to have to teach it to her, step by patient step, the way he had taught the others.

She recited the petition in archaic Val.

Perfectly.

Every syllable of it in its place — the old words, the plea for his blessing folded into them like a stone in a sling, the glottal catch before the closing phrase arriving exactly where the form demanded it, precise as a lock turning. Her voice did not shake. Her pronunciation would have satisfied a priest. She had never performed the ceremony before. She had performed it, in the warm dark of the monster's keeping, more times than she could count — into the air, into the tentacles' patient correction, module after module of it, the words and the postures and the stations drilled into her like a language she had been raised in — and her body came up off the grass now and moved through the liturgy the way water moves through a channel that was dug for it long ago.

"Хорошо♥," she whispered, to no one, to herself, in horror and in something else, as she bent to the first of his green toes and kissed it — *chu♥* — and moved, unerring, to the next.

Bog watched her with his head tilted, and his delight was a quiet, enormous thing.

"Well," he said softly, as she climbed. "Somebody's been practising."

She climbed the way she had been taught to climb, which was to say: correctly, and at the pace of a rosary.

His calves first, tree trunks in the monster's light, her hands braced against them as she rose on her knees through the cold grass. He gave her two of his fingers on the way up — an offering, unhurried — and she took them into her mouth one at a time and licked them clean as the form required, her tongue travelling around each enormous digit, while some filing clerk in her mind noted, with professional disgust, that her technique was *excellent*.

Then the testicles, and here the liturgy slowed to its true tempo. She cupped them from beneath in both hands, the way you hold something in church with both hands because one does not feel like enough respect — two warm, dense, impossible weights, each filling the side of her cheek as she pressed her lips to them, one kiss at a time. *Chu. Chu♥. Chu.* Unhurried. Salt, and clean sweat, and the fine skin sliding a little over what it held. Her bells chimed faintly at every shift of her shoulders. She had rehearsed this in the dark with nothing to hold but the idea of it, and the idea of it had been a humiliation she could survive; the reality was heavier, and warmer, and worse, because her hands knew it already.

*Baikal,* she thought, out of nowhere, the way a drowning woman thinks of a shore. *Tolbachik. Ukok.* The litany of the rodina, the frozen earth, the dead forest, the high plateau — the anchor she had carried through the whole of the regimen, name by name, like beads. *Putorana. The Lena pillars.* She kissed her way up the shaft in stations as the form demanded — lips parted, tongue flat and tasting, *chu♥, chu* — along the sides as well as the front, up the ridged and flaring length of him, thicker than her wrist, warm as something with its own weather, the great vein pulsing slow as a finger against her lips. *Baikal. Tolbachik.* The names were going soft at the edges. *Ukok — *

"Hold."

His voice, patient as a teacher's. His hand came to rest on the back of her head — not pressing. Never pressing. Just stroking, slow, the way you stroke a cat that has come in out of the rain.

"Now move. Slowly." A pause, approving. "Hold still — open. Show me."

She showed him. She moved. The wet sounds wrote themselves out into the clearing — *jupo♥ — jururu — chu♥, chu, nnn♥* — and drool escaped at the corner of her mouth and she did not wipe it, because the form did not wipe it, because her hands were where the form had put them. The training had prepared her jaw the way it had prepared everything else: she took him deep, deeper than comfort, to the back of the throat, and held, and breathed around him through her nose with the disciplined calm of a woman surfacing from a dive she had practised ten thousand times. Her eyes watered. Her pronunciation, had she been speaking, would still have been perfect.

*Putorana,* she thought, and could not remember what came after it, and found — this was the true depth of the night, deeper than his throat-stretching length in her — that she did not reach for it. The names let go of her one by one, like fingers unclenching. What was left was the heat, and the weight, and the hand stroking her hair, and the rhythm the machine had beaten into her bones.

"Good girl," Bog said, above her, quietly. "You can swallow."

She swallowed. The weight of him settled into her stomach like a stone into a pond, and warmth went through her in rings.

Then the thanksgiving: she pressed her arms against her sides and offered the prayer in the old Val tongue to the one who had provided the meal — perfect cadence, the glottal catch, the hushed, reverent drop at the end, every syllable where the monster's patience had put it. She had never said it to a living soul before. She had said it to the dark five hundred times.

Bog was silent a moment. Then his hand, which had never left her hair, stroked once more, slow and wondering.

"Perfect," he said. "Not a single mistake."

Sonya stayed where she was, kneeling in the cold grass with her forehead nearly touching his thigh, and hated — with a purity that almost comforted her — how much of her was proud.
