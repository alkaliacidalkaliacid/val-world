## Chapter 124: Fully Matured

The meal ended without incident, which was not the same as ending well.

The food was good. Dadi cooked the way she did everything, thoroughly and with standards, and the table had done it justice — Bog had done it more than justice, and said so, twice, with his mouth full. Plates were cleared. The girls were sent, protesting, in the direction of bed. Arjun was thanked for the loan of his tools and his yard. It was, by every measure anyone could say aloud, a fine homecoming dinner.

And yet.

Priya, washing up beside her mother-in-law, found her mouth watering at nothing — at the memory of a taste that was not in any dish on the table, salt and musk and green animal richness, and she swallowed, and went on scrubbing the pot as if the thought had been about the pot.

Rin, carrying plates, stopped in the doorway with her tongue pressed thoughtfully to the roof of her mouth, tasting the absence of something, and then caught herself, and stacked the plates with unnecessary precision, and did not look at Bog. She did not look at Bog twice more before bedtime, each time more deliberately than the last.

Nia ate like a woman at a state dinner. Correctly. Completely. And left the table with her jaw set and her palate aching like a pulled tooth for the one flavour her body had been rebuilt around, and if her eyes went once — once — to the head of the table, where Bog sat demolishing the last of the rice in total innocence, she took them back again like a soldier recovering a position.

None of them said a word about it. It was the only thing at the table that all three of them were thinking, and it was the one thing none of them would ever, ever say.

Then there were beds. Real beds — warm, human, mattress-and-blanket beds, in rooms with doors, in a house that smelled of cooking and soap and tulsi from the porch. After the cavern floor, after the living metal of the monster's back, after nights that had not so much been slept as endured, the whole party went into those beds the way swimmers go into still water: with a small, disbelieving sigh.

---

Priya lay in her own bed, in her own room, with her husband's arm around her, and for a while that was the whole of the world, and it was enough to make her eyes sting.

Arjun had not asked for the parts she had left out. He had held the armour, and then he had held her, and at the table he had listened to the shape of her story and nodded at the shape of it, and now, in the dark, he held her the way he had held her for nineteen years — like a man who tests everything himself first, and had tested this, and verified it: she was home, she was whole, she was his.

She turned into him. What they did then was theirs, and old, and practised, and tender in the way of things that have been done a thousand times and mean it every time — and she loved him, wholly, without reservation or performance, with the complete and undivided love of half her life.

And underneath it, traitorous as a second pulse, her body kept its own counsel.

It was not a thought. She would not allow it to become a thought. It was a physical fact, filed where physical facts go: the wanting, the specific and unmistakable wanting, for a thickness and a heat and a *taste* that this bed had never contained — the wanting that sat in her mouth and her belly like a hunger that dinner had somehow made worse. She lay in her husband's arms, loved and loving, and pretended not to notice it with all the discipline she owned.

She noticed it until she slept.

---

In the guest room, Bog was discovering that a human bed was mostly a suggestion.

He had folded himself into it as best it could be folded into — knees up, one foot on the floor for ballast, the mattress protesting in a language of springs — and Nia lay at the far edge of the available territory, straight as a ruler, facing the wall, in the posture of a woman manning a border.

Bog considered the border.

Then, with the slow, tidal inevitability of a glacier deciding to visit the sea, he began to crawl toward her.

He got perhaps six inches. He got, specifically, as far as one hand landing, soft as snowfall, on the mattress beside her hip — and Nia spoke, without turning around, in the voice she used for weather warnings and last chances.

"No."

"Just —"

"No." She turned then, and looked at him over her shoulder, and there was nothing flustered in it and nothing wavering: the Black Huntress, in a borrowed nightdress, holding a bed against a siege of one. "Not tonight. Not — " She stopped. Started again, quieter, and the quieter was worse. "I watched him hold her today. Arjun. The way he ran to her. I watched that, and I thought of Daniel, and I am not doing this tonight. Tonight I am Daniel's wife. Do you understand me? Tonight, only. But tonight."

Bog looked at her for a moment.

"Huh," he said. "Okay."

And that was all. He withdrew the hand. He resettled the glacier. He did not argue, and he did not sulk, and within a little while his breathing had gone long and even and entirely unbothered, because Bog's conscience, like Bog's appetite, was a thing that slept when it was told there was nothing for it.

Nia lay awake a while longer, in the reclaimed and chilly virtue of her one night, and told herself she had won.

---

The house went dark. The town went dark. The porch tulsi breathed its smell into the night, and the crickets took over the yard, and somewhere a dog barked twice at nothing and thought better of it.

Bog opened his eyes.

It is not widely known — it is not known at all, by anyone who has only ever seen one at rest, or at table, or in battle — that the Val are surprisingly adept at stealth. Seven and a half feet of green muscle came up off the protesting mattress without a single spring reporting it. The floorboards, consulted in sequence, kept the secret. The door, the stairs, the sleeping house, the yard: Bog crossed them all like weather moving through, silent as a held breath, and if any floorboard in Florence had an opinion about the matter it was never asked to give it.

The woods received him. The kudzu received him. And the monster, which had been lying in the green dark with its hatches closed and its household accounted for, opened one eye of light along its flank as he came through the trees — not in alarm. In welcome.

"Commander," it said, very quietly, so as not to trouble the town. "You are abroad late."

"Couldn't sleep," Bog said, which was not true, and the monster, which kept logs of everything, did not trouble the record with the discrepancy.

He walked along the great flank to the hatch he had closed, that morning, on a face full of Russian. He stood a moment with his hand on it. Inside, the regimen had been running, uninterrupted, at his own order — the hymns, the cheers, the courteous and endless attention of the thing that kept her. He had a first consort who had closed her border for the night. He had a third and a fourth asleep in warm beds, pretending not to want him. And he had a second, who had been in the keeping of the most devoted machine in the world for longer than anyone had bothered to count, being sung to, and fed, and worked on, and sung to again.

It was time, Bog decided, to tend to his second consort as well.

"Report," he said.

"Second Consort," said the monster, with the deep, clerical satisfaction of a record completing itself. "Fully matured."

Bog opened the hatch.
