## Chapter 50: Clobberin' Time


The first thing to die was the fuel drum.

It did not die so much as become a column of orange light, and the shockwave knocked three contractors flat and sent Sergeant Gorrak's roasted beetle into the dark. Then the treeline came apart. A pine the width of a truck went over sideways, and something stepped through the gap where it had stood.

It was a powered suit, but not a Dominion one. It was nine meters of salvaged plating in mismatched colors, with a school-bus door welded over one shoulder and a sticker of a cartoon cat on the chest, still bright under the floodlights. The cockpit glass sat in the middle of the chest like a cyclops's eye, and behind it, in a pair of oversized goggles, was a girl of maybe twenty with her hair in a lopsided ponytail.

She was laughing, and she did not stop even as she raised the right fist.

"It's clobberin' time!"

The fist came down on the Val field kitchen. The field kitchen stopped being a field kitchen.

"HOLY VAL!" Gorrak bellowed from where he was lying. "Contact! *Contact!* Is that the Old Guards?"

Nobody answered him, because the answer arrived in the form of gunfire. Humans came out of the trees on both flanks, a dozen, then twenty. They wore camouflage jackets, hunting vests, and a few pieces of stolen Dominion armor. They carried rifles, shotguns, and one grim-faced grandmother with a belt-fed machine gun, and every one of them wore a clean white armband stitched with a red cross and a number.

Under the tent canvas, Nia heard the shooting begin and went completely still.

*Armbands.* She had not seen armbands since the first month of the war. Somebody out there, in the ruins of the world, was still insisting on being a lawful combatant. She almost laughed. It would have been a terrible sound, and the soldier on top of her would have taken it for the wrong thing entirely.

He did not take it for anything. He had stopped moving at the first explosion and was staring toward the flap with his trousers around his knees and an expression of pure, stupid betrayal.

"Sir," said a voice from outside. "Sir, we're being *attacked.*"

"I can hear that!" the soldier snapped. "I'm on my *turn!*"

"There's a robot, sir. A big one. It has a *cat* on it."

Nia, whose hands were cuffed in front of her and whose entire body was still humming from the last palm print, did the only reasonable thing available. She drew both knees to her chest and drove them into his chest with everything she had left.

It was not enough, with the collar on. It was enough. Two hundred and forty kilos of Val infantryman went through the tent flap backward, taking the flap with him, and landed in the mud in a tangle of canvas and swearing.

"*Pickle,*" she hissed to nobody, and rolled off the cot.

The camp was in pieces. The suit had dropped a truck onto the dice-game crowd, and the Val were rallying behind the wreck, firing heavy rifles that spat green bolts of light. The bolts flashed off the suit's plating and left black scars. The girl in the cockpit shrieked with delight at every one. Down the slope, the armbanded humans fought in disciplined pairs, ten seconds of fire and then a shift, the way the old Accord manuals taught it. A Val sergeant who tried to charge them took six rounds in the knees from six different directions and sat down in surprise.

The human contractors were the ones in real trouble. They had been standing in the open with their rifles pointed in the wrong direction, and now they were realizing which side of the line they were on. The thin man with the cracked radio dropped his weapon and raised both hands high. A boy with an armband, scarcely sixteen, looked at him over the sights of a hunting rifle, then past him, and lowered it.

Nia crawled. The ground shook under her hands, bells chiming with every lurch, a sweet and merry sound in the middle of hell. She did not care. The supply stack was fifteen meters away. The gray case sat on top of a ration crate, exactly where she had last seen it, and nobody was guarding it now, because every Val in the camp was busy dying or yelling.

She was ten meters away when a shadow fell across her.

Gorrak stood over her, breathing hard, with a green-black burn across his scalp and a dull orange gleam in his eyes. He had lost the beetle, the dice game, and his entire evening, and in his enormous hand was the loose end of the leash the camp had clipped to her collar.

"No, no, no," he said. "You're *mine,* little rabbit. Finders keepers."

Behind him, the nine-meter suit turned its head.
