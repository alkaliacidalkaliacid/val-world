## Chapter 93: A Working Arrangement

The talk lasted less than a minute, and Nia was the one who cut it short.

"Listen to me," she said. "My friend Rin — the girl who carried me up here — she's fallen into a cave under this mountain. The pick—" She stopped. Cleared her throat. "Bog has gone down after her. That was some time ago, and there's been no sign of either of them since."

Priya sat up slowly, Bog's coat sliding off her shoulder. She looked toward the slope, where the purple light had stood, and then back at Nia, and her eyes were brown and frightened and entirely her own, and that was exactly why Nia could stop watching them.

"I'm going to find a way down," Nia said. "You wait here. You've done enough. Rest."

"W-wait here?" Priya's hands twisted in her lap. "Alone? Nia, no. No, I c-can't— if something comes up out of the ground again, if the light—" She shook her head, hard, the way a person shakes off water. "I am coming with you. I am a n-nurse. If your friend is hurt down there, if *he* is hurt— you will need hands that know what to do with a wound. Mine know."

Nia looked at her for a moment.

"Stay where I can see you," she said, and turned away down the slope before the woman could thank her.

They searched the way people search a shoreline, in slow arcs, heads down. The mountain was wrong in the small ways now — no birds, grass that leaned all one way like hair in a current — but the stone was only stone, warm from a sun that felt very far away. Twice Nia found cracks that promised depth and gave her back nothing but her own echo and a smell of wet rock. The third time she straightened up too fast and the bells at her breasts rang, bright and stupid in the quiet, and she pressed a hand flat over them through the overalls and stood there hating every separate piece of metal her body had been fitted with.

It was not only the bells. It was the rings, catching as she climbed. It was the string of beads, shifting with every step, a small obscene reminder with a rhythm like a pulse. And under all of it, growing the way thirst grows — not an event, a condition — was the hunger.

She had gone too long without him. That was the plain arithmetic of it, and her body kept doing the sum over and over and presenting her with the total. Her mouth watered at nothing. Her thighs pressed together when she crouched, of their own accord, traitors. She thought about Bog's hands, Bog's smell, the particular green weight of him, and the wanting went through her like a draught through a house, opening doors.

"Nia."

She turned too quickly. Priya had stopped a few paces back and was watching her with her head a little tilted — not frightened now. Clinical. It was the look of a woman who has taken a great many pulses and learned to hear the ones people try to hide.

"You are not well," Priya said. "Your color is wrong and you keep— you are in some kind of pain, I think. Or distress. I cannot tell which, and I do not like not being able to tell."

"I'm fine."

"You are not fine. You are—" Priya hesitated, and then, gently, in the tone of someone setting down something breakable: "M-May I ask you something? What are you to him? To Bog. The way you watch the ground for him. The way you said his name just now, after the other word. What are you t-to him?"

The bells betrayed her first — her whole body had gone hot and the little movement of her breathing set them off, a soft, mortifying music.

"I'm his—" She swallowed. "It's an arrangement. A working arrangement. He travels, I— it's complicated, and it's not— Priya, it is not any of your—"

"Of c-course," Priya said quickly, palms up, retreating at once. "Of course. Forgive me. It is not my business."

Nia stood there with her face burning and her heart going like a fist on a door, and because she could not stand being the only exposed thing on the mountain, she fired the question back.

"Fine. Then what is he to *you*? You talk about him like— back there, you said his name like a prayer. What is Bog to you, Priya?"

Priya's face changed. The timidity went out of it the way a tide goes out — not gradually, just gone — and what was left was simple and a little fierce.

"He kept our clinic alive," she said. "For two years the medicines came and no one would say from where. Val medicines, Nia — antibiotics we could not make, things for the children, for the fevers. My Arjun found out first. An anonymous supplier, taking terrible risks, asking almost n-nothing. And then the bear came." Her voice did not shake now. "You have heard what the bears are, since the war. This one had a dead man's voice in it. It had my husband by— it *took* him, it wore him, and Bog held his throat in his hands and would not close them. A Val, with every reason. He would not do it. He fought that thing with his head, with his hands, like a man in a bar fight, and my daughters were in the house, and my husband's mother—" She stopped, and pressed her lips together, and nodded once, as if confirming a measurement. "My family is alive. All of them. That is what he is to me."

Nia said nothing. There was a version of Bog she could have laid next to this one — the markets, the money he skimmed off every crate that had ever passed through his hands, the profit in those anonymous deliveries — and she found she had no way to say any of it that did not begin with what she was to him, and she had already declined to say that.

So she said the other true thing instead.

"He's a lecher," she said. "Whatever else he is. You should know it. He's a filthy-minded, hands-everywhere lecher, and one day he'll— Priya. I'm warning you. Keep your distance from that side of him."

Priya stared at her. Then the nurse laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, one hand flying to her mouth.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, Nia. I have been m-married nineteen years, and I work in a clinic. Men are—" She shook her head, still smiling, fond and utterly dismissive. "He has been nothing but c-courteous to me. A gentleman, even. You must not tease me with such stories."

It was at that moment, with Nia standing open-mouthed in the wreckage of her warning, that Priya's foot slipped on the scree — and did not stop slipping. She sat down hard, slid a yard, and caught herself at the lip of a crack in the mountainside that neither of them had seen: a long black seam running down out of the light, breathing cold air up into the heat of the day.

They looked at each other. Then they both looked down into the dark.

From far below, or from nowhere, or from the metal at her own breast, came the faintest sound of a bell.
