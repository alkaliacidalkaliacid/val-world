## Chapter 30: Separation


"Тебя убью!" Sonya screamed.

"Got that part," Bog said.

A disc flared open above his head, wide enough to drop something heavy through. He caught the glint of it, a sliver of an overhead gantry lined up with the portal's mouth, and had just enough time to think, *Oh, come on…*

A tentacle hit her from the side.

It struck with a crack like a whip. The portal collapsed, and the girder that had been poised above Bog's skull dropped three feet and thudded harmlessly to the floor. Sonya went tumbling, rolled, and brought up another disc in a single motion. The tentacle was severed at the root and fell in twitching coils. She stepped into a disc and was gone.

She reappeared twenty feet away, and a second tentacle had been waiting. It seized her round the waist and slammed her onto the deck with a sound that shook dust from the ceiling.

Bog watched with his head tilted and his arm hanging like a wet rag.

"Aren't the two of you tired of this yet?" he muttered.

He pushed himself to his feet and promptly tripped over something metal. He caught himself on a crate, swore, and looked down. A collar lay in the rubble, a spare, unlocked, its dull gray band still gleaming with factory oil. He had no use for it just now. He picked it up anyway, because it was lying there, and hooked it onto his belt with the vague thought that it might come in handy.

Then he looked up and spotted the pod.

It sat at the far end of the bay, half-hidden behind a toppled rack of armored lockers: squat, bullet-shaped, its status light blinking green. The pod from the cradle row. The one that had vanished in front of his eyes. Its hatch was shut now, and sealed — she had tucked it away here, out of the monster's reach, for herself. Bog stared at it, then at the woman fighting for her life in the middle of the hall.

"I *knew* it," he said, almost admiring. "You were saving that for yourself, weren't you?"

He ran. Nia was still on her feet by the pillar, and he scooped her up with his one working arm and carried her the whole way. She let out a startled sound and the bells rang. He hauled the hatch open with his shoulder and tossed her inside, toolkit and all.

"Bog!"

"This'll do. Look, it's powering up."

The console flickered. A soft whine climbed in the walls, and the seat harnesses snapped across her chest on their own. Nia twisted around to look at him through the closing gap, the red alarm light striping his face.

"What about you?"

"Take the toolkit to Dr. Hermes," Bog said. "At the Free Clinic's Appalachian branch. You know the place."

"*Bog…*"

He slammed the hatch.

The locks engaged with a heavy click. Through the little round viewport she saw him thump the outside of the pod twice with his good fist, like a man patting a horse, and turn away before she could say anything else. There was no look back, no parting speech. The Val had no word for self-sacrifice, and Bog would have blinked at the human one. He had sent the important cargo on its way; his own pod was still out there, and watching hers launch would not bring it any closer.

The pod shuddered and began to slide.

Bog was already sprinting toward the next one.

It sat in a neighboring bay, hatch ajar, a smaller, battered model with scorch marks along its side. He dove in headfirst, scrabbling for the lever, and had just gotten the hatch half-closed when the world went dark around him.

A tentacle the width of a tree trunk wrapped around the pod and *squeezed*.

"Seriously…?"

The pod spun. The walls groaned. Through the viewport he saw cables sliding past like the walls of a throat, glistening and coiling, and then he was inside the creature, swallowed whole in a dark, wet world of grinding metal.

For a moment there was only the roaring in his ears and the smell of burnt oil.

Then the whole world lurched.

It wasn't the creature. It was the warehouse. A tremor ran through the floor, up the bulkheads, into the monster, and into the little pod caught in its belly, a deep, boneshaking rumble like a mountain deciding to move. Bog braced his boots against the console and grabbed a strap with his good hand. Alarms screamed overhead.

"I don't know what's happening," he said to nobody, "but I've got a really, *really* bad feeling about this."

He was right.

In the ruined depot, Sonya stood over the wreck of her plan with blood on her chin and every nerve in her body burning, and she did the last thing she had left in her. A hundred discs bloomed around the monster at once, every one she could hold, and snapped shut in a single blinding instant.

The cut was perfect. It ran through the deck, the ribs of the hull, and the gantries holding the creature's weight, and it severed the thing from the half-destroyed warehouse like a splinter pulled from a thumb. For a moment the whole mass hung in the open dark of space, a bloated knot of steel cables the size of a barge with a tiny pod in its stomach, flailing its limbs at nothing.

Then the run-of-the-mill bomb went off.

It did not do what it said on the casing — centuries in a dusty storehouse had seen to that — but what it did was more than enough. The blast burned away every part of the creature still tangled in the depot's bones, and kicked what was left, the great severed knot of it, out of the dark and Earthward.

It fell, tumbling end over end, trailing fire and wreckage and a faint wail of tearing metal, as the blue curve of the Earth rose up to meet it.
