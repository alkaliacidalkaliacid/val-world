## Chapter 123: Good for Them

The monster stopped at the edge of Florence and would go no further.

It settled into the woods outside the town, down among the trees where the kudzu ran thick over everything the war had left, and lowered itself into the green shade with the patience of a thing that had nowhere else to be. From its back the town was visible in pieces — rooftops, a water tower, the slow brown shine of the Tennessee River beyond — and it was close enough that Bog could have walked in before the heat of the day was done.

Which raised a problem, and Nia raised it.

"Those two," she said, "are not walking into a town naked."

Rin had been naked since the cavern and had made her peace with it. Priya had been naked longer and had not. Both of them looked down at themselves, and then at Nia, who stood there in her loose overalls with her arms folded, in the unanswerable position of the only dressed woman in the party.

The monster considered the matter in the way it considered everything, thoroughly and aloud. It had, it reported, a solution in keeping with the household's dignity. There were suits in the stores it had taken up from the depot. Armoured suits, rated for vacuum and for war, Val issue.

What it produced, unfolding out of its own flank like a wardrobe deciding to be generous, were two armoured spacesuits built for soldiers of Bog's people.

Bog's people were seven and a half feet tall.

The suits were magnificent. The suits were also, on Priya and on Rin, a catastrophe of scale. The breastplates hung to their knees. The gauntlets swallowed their arms to the elbow and left their fingers feeling for the controls somewhere in the dark beyond the wrist. The boots were buckets with ambitions. When Rin took her first step the whole suit rang softly, like a bell that had been taught to walk, and she had to be caught by a tentacle before she went over backwards into the grass.

"It is very safe," the monster said, with enormous satisfaction. "Nothing can harm you inside it."

"I can't *see*," Rin said, from somewhere inside the helmet, which had settled down over her eyes. A tentacle reached in, courteously, and tipped the helmet back and wedged it. She emerged blinking, chin tipped up, peering out from under the rim like a turtle that had been given a promotion. "Okay. Okay, I can work with this."

Priya said nothing at all. Priya was standing very still, in the posture of a woman conducting a private negotiation with gravity, both arms slightly out, the suit ticking faintly as it settled its weight across her shoulders. She took one step. The suit took it with her, a half-second late, and clanked.

So they came into Florence: Bog first, unhurried, a green giant in the summer street; then Nia, tall and robust and dark in her overalls, walking like a woman escorting a parade she had not agreed to; and behind them, clanking, swaying, stopping every few paces to renegotiate with their own boots, a broad South Asian woman and a slight Asian girl inside two suits of Val war armour that were each, roughly, a small room with legs.

Florence stopped to watch. A man came out of a doorway with a crate in his arms and simply stood there holding it. Two boys abandoned a bicycle in the road and followed at a worshipful distance, the way boys follow anything that clanks. An old woman on a porch crossed herself, not in fear — in recognition of effort.

Priya kept her eyes forward and walked like a woman bringing herself home through sheer administration. *Left. Clank. Right. Clank.* The clinic was ahead. Her street was ahead. Her house was ahead, with the marigolds and the tulsi by the porch, and she was nearly there, she was nearly —

"Priya!"

Arjun came out of his workshop at a run.

He did not slow down for the armour. It has to be understood that he never even appeared to see it: not the breastplate, not the helmet riding down over her ears, not the fact that his wife had come home from a war with the spirits dressed as a piece of siege equipment. He crossed the yard at full speed, got both arms around the suit — they did not meet at the back; the suit was too wide; he hugged it anyway, hard enough to make it ring — and said her name into the side of the helmet like a man talking through the door of a house he had been locked out of.

"You're home," he said. "You're home, you're — let me look at you, no, hold still, you're *home* —"

The helmet would not turn properly. Priya turned the whole suit instead, in small clanking increments, and got her gauntlets up and onto his shoulders, and the two of them stood there in the yard holding on to each other, him laughing with his forehead against the armour and her making a sound inside it that was not quite either of the things it wanted to be.

Then the screen door banged, and Meera and Kavya came off the porch at a run, and the armour acquired two more people, one on each side, hanging off it like swimmers off a raft.

"Good for them," Bog said.

Beside him, inside the second suit, Rin nodded — vigorously, with her whole upper body, so that the suit nodded with her, helmet dipping and rising like a buoy in a swell. *Clank. Clank. Clank.*

Nia looked at Bog.

She did not say anything. She had a way, when the words were inadequate, of simply presenting him with the fact of her face, and she presented it now: the level stare, the scarred cheek, the patience of a woman keeping a ledger in which this, too, had just been entered. Bog, who had survived the look a hundred times, survived it again, and beamed at the reunion like a man at a wedding he had personally catered.

---

They got the suits off in the yard, in the end, with Arjun's tools and the monster's distant, radioed advice and a great deal of profanity in three languages, and then there was a meal, because in that house a return was a thing you fed.

Priya told it at the table, with her hands around a cup, in the voice she used for family: the stammer in it, the words coming in their own order. The thing at the mountain — the buried thing, the source of the bhutas — it was d-done. Sealed. She had done what she went to do. She did not say how. She did not say what had spoken through her while she did it, or what it had promised to do to the human race afterward, or what had been done, in a dark cavern, to stop it. She did not mention collars, or counts, or the word *fourth*. She said the mission was accomplished, and that she was home, and both of those were true, and she let them be the whole of it.

Bog, halfway through the meal, reached along the back of Nia's chair, draped one enormous arm around her shoulders, and hauled her sideways into his side with the contentment of a man settling a cushion.

Nia ground her teeth.

She stayed where she had been put. It was a public table, and he was behaving, by his standards, like a gentleman: an arm, a shoulder, nothing lower and nothing wandering. She had a clear and recently refreshed knowledge of the alternatives, and she sat inside the arm and ground her teeth and ate her dinner like a woman making the best of a treaty.

Across the table, Dadi watched it all over the rim of her glasses.

The old woman's face had been a closed door since Bog sat down. Now, slowly, something in it eased. She looked at the arm. She looked at the big dark woman sitting inside it, upright and furious and eating. She drew the obvious conclusion, which was the wrong one: the woman was his. His wife. The Val had a wife of his own — a great strapping wife, who sat at his side at another family's table and was pulled against him in front of everyone, a bit much, that, in someone else's house, but there it was.

It did not make her like him. It did not make her trust him. But the thing she had braced for, all this time — the prowling, the eyeing, the alien with his hands and his reputation loose in the house around her son's wife — that, perhaps, she had misjudged. A man with a woman of his own was a different shape of problem. Her shoulders came down a fraction. She reached, after a moment, and pushed the dish of raita an inch in Bog's direction.

It was, where her precious daughter-in-law was concerned, about as far from the truth as it was possible to get.

---

The girls, meanwhile, had Rin.

They had her the way weather has a roof. Meera hung back at first, in her way, watching; Kavya did not hang back at all, and by the time the plates were cleared the two of them had Rin cornered by the porch steps in a state of total journalistic occupation.

"What's it like in Mississippi?"

"Hot," Rin said. "And the river's *huge*, and everything's broken, and the mosquitoes — okay, the mosquitoes are like this —" She held her hands apart. "No, bigger. And there was a bear. Sort of. It's a long story."

"Can you do kung fu?"

"Can I — " Rin lit up like a struck match. "Okay, so, technically my suit does most of the — I mean, I *trained*, there's forms, my master made me do forms until I wanted to die, watch —"

She got up to demonstrate, in the yard, in the dusk, with the wrecked ghost of a powered suit nowhere on her and nothing but enthusiasm to work with, and she put her whole heart into a stance that owed as much to cinema as to any master, while two teenage girls watched her with the complete, uncritical reverence of an audience that has found its person.

She told them about the river, and the bear, and the road. She did not tell them who her master was, or what she had been doing in Mississippi, or whose records she could be found in, and the questions, being a child's questions, never once went anywhere near it.

---

Did we forget anyone?

Out in the woods, in the green dark under the trees, the monster lay at rest with its hatches closed and its household accounted for, and in a compartment in its flesh a woman lay snared in silk and cable, in the warm and the wet and the endless, courteous attention of the thing that kept her.

"Orgasm count," the monster said, quietly, to its own records. "Thirty-one. Consecutive."

From inside the compartment, muffled, furious, and not entirely furious, came a voice: "Пошёл ты♥ —"

"Logged," said the monster, "as encouragement."
