## Chapter 39: Landfall


The monster hit the Gulf like a meteor, and the sea closed over it with a roar that Bog felt through the soles of his boots. Then the roar faded, and there was only the long, muffled hush of deep water.

It did not stop. The creature walked the ocean floor on a thousand cable-legs, dragging a plume of silt behind it, past drowned highways and the black ribs of sunken ships, until the slope began to rise toward shore. Somewhere near the ruins that had once been the city of Mobile, it found soft ground, broke the surface of the earth, and burrowed in. The pod in its belly tilted, steadied, and settled. Water drained out of the walls.

"Landing complete, Commander," the voice said.

"Huh." Bog sat in the silence for a while with his good hand on his chest. He was alive. He had fallen out of orbit inside a living weapon and he was alive. "Huh," he said again, with deep respect for himself.

His left arm had begun to tingle. He flexed it and winced.

"All right, squirmy, wriggly monster, what've you got in there? Make me an inventory."

A screen lit on the dead console, and a list began to scroll: ordnance, vehicles, fuel cells, bolts of uniform cloth, crates of expired ration bars, some of it intact and stacked neatly in the monster's gut, some of it already digested into the cables of its body. Bog skimmed with his head tilted, muttering "Useless… useless… ooh, machine oil…" Then he stopped.

Piercing-rings. Bells. A dozen strings of beads, in assorted models.

"Well, well." He scratched his jaw. "Fit them on the prisoner. All of them. Nicely."

He did not watch. He leaned back and closed his eyes and let the machine go about its work, and through the walls of the pod, after a while, came a small, bright sound, a clear, lovely chiming that rose and fell and rose again. It was the same sound that Nia's body made when she walked. The Val had designed it to be pleasant. It was.

On a cradle of woven cable deep in the creature's belly, the Pale Murderess lay with her silver hair plastered to her forehead and her eyes half-open, empty of focus. A gray collar sat at her throat. Rings and tiny bells had been fitted at her breasts and elsewhere with a surgeon's neatness, and her body gave one faint tremor each time the tentacles shifted her, and each tremor chimed.

"Done, Commander."

"Good. Good." Bog opened one eye. "Now, about her meals. I want what she eats to be exactly the same composition as my own seed. Same formula. Same everything."

"I will require a sample."

Bog's other eye opened.

"Of course you will," he said slowly. He rubbed his face with his good hand, the way a man does when he has a bad idea and knows it and has decided, without much struggle, to go ahead. "Fine. Fine. But I'm not doing this with *you*, you understand. Make me something nicer to look at."

"Specify the form."

Bog hesitated. He thought of a woman with close-cropped hair, a scar from temple to cheek, and a mouth that never stopped cursing him. Then he said it before the thought could take root.

"Nia. The stunning beauty who was with me at the depot! With a big butt and big breasts, and smooth, dark skin..."

The cables in front of him knotted, rippled, and drew up into a column. Muscle formed along the length of it, then shoulders, a long back, a heavy, powerful frame. A face came last, with a scar running down the left cheek. Well, It put in a good effort. The creature had had a long look at Nia on the way in. The eyes opened, brown and wary. Tiny bells chimed on the copy's chest, because the creature had copied those too.

It walked across the pod with her stride and stopped in front of him.

Bog swallowed.

"Don't talk," he said hoarsely. "Not a word. I don't want to hear it say anything."

The copy tilted its head. Then it climbed into his lap, and the pod went quiet except for the bells.

---

"Sample acquired," the voice said some time later. "Analysis complete. Composition replicated at one hundred percent."

Bog sat in the dim light with his head thrown back and his eyes on the ceiling, breathing slowly. The copy had already melted back into the cables, and the pod looked as if it had never been there.

"Right," he said at last, hoarse and distracted. "Good. Start the meals."

Then, quietly, to himself: "…Don't tell her about this part."
