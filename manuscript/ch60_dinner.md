## Chapter 60: Dinner


The dining table was a door laid across two sawhorses, and every inch of it was covered. There was a pot of dal, a stack of rotis wrapped in a dish towel, rice heaped in a dented steel bowl, and an aloo gobi still hissing from the pan. A chicken curry the color of a sunset sat in the middle, with a jar of fiery pickle beside it that was clearly meant as a dare.

Bog sat at the head of the table because it was the only chair that could take his weight. His knees stuck out on both sides.

"Please," said Priya's husband, Arjun, a thin man with grease permanently ground into the creases of his knuckles. "Eat. Eat as much as you like."

"Don't mind if I do."

He did not mind. He tore a roti in half, scooped up curry with it, and swallowed the whole thing in one go. Across the table, the two teenage daughters watched him with the flat, narrowed look of girls who had been warned about men like this. The younger one, Kavya, held her fork like a weapon. The elder, Meera, had not touched her plate.

At the far end sat the mother-in-law, a tiny woman in a faded green sari. She glared at him over her spectacles like a customs officer who had found something in the luggage.

"Dadi," Priya murmured. "Please."

"I am eating," said the old woman. "I am allowed to look while I eat."

Bog took a spoonful of the pickle and chewed thoughtfully. His eyes watered. He nodded, deeply moved.

"This is good. This is *really* good. What is this?"

"Lime," said Arjun. "And green chili. My mother makes it."

"Your mother." Bog turned toward the end of the table. "Ma'am, I salute you. In my whole career I have never been this angry at a vegetable and enjoyed it this much."

Dadi's glare did not move. But her chin lifted a fraction of an inch.

"Do you have a mother?" Kavya asked suddenly. It sounded less like conversation than like an accusation.

"Kavya," Priya hissed.

"Nope," said Bog cheerfully, reaching for the rice. "Never did. We come out of a cradle. A big machine, lots of tubes, very efficient. Though the cradles are broken now." He frowned and added, as an afterthought, "Something got in them."

The table went quiet. Meera finally looked up. Arjun's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"No mother," Dadi repeated slowly.

"No, ma'am."

"No one to cook for you?"

"Well, there's the mess hall."

The old woman said something short and sharp in Hindi. Priya flinched. Arjun covered his mouth with his hand, and it was unclear whether he was hiding a laugh or a wince.

"What did she say?" Bog asked.

"Sh-She said," Priya began carefully, "that it explains a great deal."

"Ha!" Bog slapped the table so hard the dal jumped. "I like her."

"Don't," said Dadi.

He helped himself to more curry. Between mouthfuls he was an easy guest, praising the rice for its fluffiness and asking Arjun how a man with those hands had fixed Val medical equipment. Arjun answered with the shy pride of a craftsman who rarely got an audience. Bit by bit the room loosened. Kavya let her fork drop to the plate. Meera pushed a small bowl of cucumber raita an inch toward the giant without looking at him, which in her language seemed to be a treaty.

Bog tore off one last roti, wiped the pot clean, and sat back with his hands on his belly. Then he let out a belch that began in his boots and ended somewhere near the ceiling. A cup rolled off the table. Kavya burst into laughter before she could stop herself, and clapped a hand over her mouth, scandalized.

"Excellent," Bog sighed. "Ten out of ten. Only thing missing is a cold beer."

"There is no beer," said Dadi, with some satisfaction.

"I noticed."

He reached into the pickle jar one more time, then paused. He held a single glistening green chili between two thick fingers and studied it for a long moment. The thought was gone before he could name it. Something about a woman who liked her peppers hot, and a kitchen somewhere with a lemon-print apron on a hook.

He ate the chili himself.

Outside, the evening settled over Florence. Somewhere past the fence line a dog began to bark, then stopped abruptly, mid-note, as though a hand had closed around its throat.

Priya's eyes went to the window. So did Dadi's.

Neither of them said a word.
