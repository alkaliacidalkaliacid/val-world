- Grounding: Second-generation Igbo immigrant, born and raised in Baltimore in the clan's apartment complex ("Cold Spring Courts") — never wealthy, firmly clear of crime. The spirits guard her clan, meaning the people welcomed into that community, not bloodline alone, and a heavily blessed one like her appears from time to time. ### Daniel, Amara, Chidi
- Daniel Okonkwo was her first and only love (user ruling). They met when she was 24, carrying a rescued, injured child into the University of Maryland ER where he was a resident; married at 25; Amara was born when she was 26, Chidi when she was 30. A modest Poconos weekend was all the honeymoon they could afford, remembered fondly and with a little rue. They quote *Coming to America* at each other, line for line.
- The marriage in small things: Daniel cannot take heat — "pepper is a condiment, not an ingredient" — so she portions his share out first, complaining every time, and does it every time. He owns the oven and breakfast; she owns the stove. The physician nags about salt and oil; she ignores him, and nags him about his health while shelving her own recklessness. Early in the marriage she hid hero injuries from him, which caused their biggest fight — the "reconnaissance" excuse habit started at home.
- As a mother: Amara at seven rejected her egusi ("slimy") and genuinely wounded her; at twelve she asked to learn jollof, which was the reconciliation and the handover — and the crispy bottom of the pot is the family's standing squabble. Chidi inherited her heat tolerance and is her appointed taster ("approved," he declares). She once missed Amara's recital for a rescue; Amara still holds it.
- The separation: The family was split in the fall of the eastern seaboard. She believes they may still be alive, refuses to discuss them, and asked Bog not to look them up. Her private litany holds the two halves of her together: "tomato, Scotch bonnet, thyme; Amara, Chidi, Daniel."

### The hero's trajectory
- She awakened at fifteen, as "Dike Nwanyi", in a large fire at Cold Spring Courts — her own community in crisis, which is what a clan hero is for — rescuing a child and carrying the collapsing building on her back (ch07; a pre-war episode, unrelated to the invasion). A student hero through high school (Baltimore Polytechnic; strong at mathematics and biology) and community college; the semester after her awakening, her grades collapsed from rescue absences. An early rescue that put a robber in hospital taught her control of her strength.
- The scar from her left temple to her cheek is from the Lancet, a blade-telekinetic villain whose cuts refuse healing, taken shielding a patient in a hospital hostage incident; even her super-recovery cannot erase it.
- In the invasion she held the Baltimore evacuation corridor for nine days — 30,000 escaped — before defeat and capture. After capture: forced service to over 400 Val males, her spiritual protection (she can conceive only by a male she truly recognises as a suitable mate — her spirits judge a mate by what he does when nobody keeps score) frustrating the Val into neglect and harsh treatment, until Bog took personal ownership of her as a condition of his transfer to the reserves, and tamed her through affection rather than force.

### Food and cooking
- Lineages: Igbo home cooking from her mother is the main line. Cooking is how she protects people when she is not fighting: on Sundays she cooked for the whole building, and a sick neighbour meant her arriving with a pot. Baltimore steamed crabs with Old Bay she made her own.
- Her rules: Her litany — "tomato, Scotch bonnet, thyme" — is the opening of the cooking itself, the base nearly everything starts from: the tomato fried until the oil separates, thyme never omitted, nothing measured — quantities live in her hands, not in her mother's handwritten notebook. Jollof must have the smoky burnt bottom, and she concedes nothing in the Ghana argument. Serving order is strict: elders, children, everyone.
- Dishes and their memories: Jollof rice — her mother cooking for the courtyard; at twelve Nia was first trusted with the spoon, her mark of being grown. Pepper soup — her mother's medicine when she was feverish as a child; as an adult she carried it to Daniel's ER night shift and became a legend among his colleagues. Egusi and pounded yam — the clan's New Yam Festival (Iri ji), where after her awakening she alone pounds for everyone: the one day her strength is pure blessing.
- Learned from: Her mother, who handed her the work in order — wash, chop, stir, then the spoon. Her grandmother in Nigeria, her authority for "the real way." Her patients, recipe by recipe (see below). She is now forgetting the taste of Earth food (see Body and Conditioning), which is why the litany matters.

### The physical therapist assistant
- She trained at the Community College of Baltimore County and worked at the Kennedy Krieger Institute, in rehabilitation. The work was her ordinary life — the part of the week where nobody needed rescuing and progress came in inches — and it mattered to her precisely for that: hero work spent her strength in moments, but rehab gave it back as people slowly recovering their lives. She was good at it and knew it; her energy encouraged patients who had given up on themselves. It was also the world that bordered Daniel's — wards and referrals, medicine practised in daylight — and the one place she was simply Nia the professional, not the Huntress.
- A construction worker told he would never walk again walked at his daughter's wedding because she out-stubborned his despair. She was once written up for lifting a patient alone, against protocol — the same rule-bending as her hero work, aimed at the person in front of her.
- The job is also where her two halves met: the cool, dry professional the patients trusted — dry humour and stubbornness in equal measure — who over long sessions traded recipes with them like family, and remembers them by their recipes still.
