## A. Contradictions with canon / settings
No hard contradictions. The chapter's load-bearing facts check out unusually well against later chapters (see the verified points at the end of this section). Items:

- **A1 (low, documentation) - The clinic siphoning is paid business, and later prose confirms it.** Bog: "I siphon a bit of medical supply off to their Appalachia branch. They love Val gear. **They pay a fortune.**" And in the final scene: "I don't take these kinds of risks **just for profit**." Bog's card records the diversion neutrally ("secretly diverts Val medical supplies"), and the ch001 ruling deliberately left Bog's *black-market* profit ambiguous. The Clinics are a different channel, and ch056 settles this one on the page: Priya calls him "the volunteer who keeps bringing us medical supplies", and Bog corrects her - "Business, actually." So ch008 and ch056 agree: the siphoning is commercial. Recommend recording that on Bog's card (Clinics pay; it is business, not charity), explicitly distinct from the still-ambiguous black-market question. No prose change.
- **A2 (low) - The Tijuana cartel plan never recurs in this form.** As Bog tells it here: a drug cartel in Tijuana, run by a supervillain in hiding, desperate for Val weapons; he will leak a route, cause chaos, and slip into the fortress. What actually happens (ch020-ch022, recounted in ch054): Bog incites the Broker's people, who hire a supervillain (Sonya) to raid the orbital depot. No Tijuana, no cartel, and the supervillain is hired muscle, not the cartel's boss. This reads as plan-vs-reality drift rather than contradiction - ch021 explicitly establishes that half of Bog's fortress lore came from a drunk quartermaster and market rumor, and Nia's objection in this very chapter ("You'd get a whole cartel killed over a box of tools") may be why the plan changed shape. Flagged so the pass treats ch008's version as Bog's early rumor-plan, superseded, not as a fact about the world. No change proposed.
- **A3 (low) - Collar removal by technicians vs. "no reliable way".** Nia's deliberation: "Collars like mine aren't taken off by privates. They're locked and unlocked by a narrow caste of technicians, working from manuals none of them can read, and the Free Clinics have spent years failing to open a single one without killing the wearer." Canon: no reliable way to disable or remove a collar exists yet, and the Val barely understand their inherited tech. The two reconcile if the technicians' unlocking is an *institutional* capability (authorized tools, authorized hands - exactly what Hermes says he could do with the depot tools) while no method exists for the resistance or for safe unauthorized removal. Her skepticism ("What key does he think he's holding?") is also the deal's engine: Bog is promising more than a private should be able to deliver, and T01 / ch012 keep that weak point alive. No change proposed; flagged so later chapters are read on the institutional reading.

## B. Internal consistency and character voice

- **B1 (main point, craft/clarity) - The deal itself is never proposed on the page.** The chapter's central event - Bog's actual proposal - happens entirely off-page, inside the cut between Nia's "Please" and her deliberation interior. The reader never hears Bog state the terms. What the terms *are* must be assembled from her thoughts: "A proper, devoted wife" is the price; he will take the collar off (implied by "He's offering me the door" and her technician objection); she may go and find her family herself ("He didn't say he'd look for them. He said I could go and find them myself"); the secrecy is a quoted fragment ("From the others, Earth or Val"). The signal code - "One long slurp for a deal. Two for no" - likewise appears as her recollection of an instruction the reader never saw given (see B2). This is the ch006/ch007 pattern (decisive Bog lines arriving reported, not shown), but here the elided speech is the book's central contract, whose exact terms T01, ch012 and the whole raid plot hang on. The deliberation interior is superb, and a reader *can* reconstruct the terms - but this is the one ellipsis in the book so far that costs clarity. Options: (a) surface the proposal in a short narration bridge or a few lines of Bog dialogue before the deliberation (the ch006 fix, applied to the deal), stating the four terms and the code; (b) keep the ellipsis deliberately and record here that the terms are established by her interior plus ch012. Author's call; no recommendation between them, but it should be a decision, not an accident.
- **B2 (linked to B1) - The signal code has no origin scene.** "One long slurp for a deal. Two for no." is recorded in chapters/ch008.md as an established fact and is the mechanism of the chapter's climax (the very long slurp), yet within the prose nobody is shown proposing the code. If B1(a) is chosen, one clause in the bridge fixes both. Note also: the code is never used again in the later prose checked (her later answers are speech and ♥) - it functions here as a one-scene device that converts the meal into language, which is thematically enough.
- **B3 (minor, staging) - Couch or kitchen?** The opening scene is staged on the couch ("Bog's back arched against the couch cushions", her cheek on his thigh, the beer bottle on the table) and then locates itself in the kitchen ("Her breath was the only sound in the kitchen", the dried egg crumb by the table leg). The third scene repeats the pattern (bathroom mirror, then "Bog's lap... knees sunk into the couch cushions"). The relay station is small and the rooms may be effectively open-plan (ch005 already had Bog on the couch listening to her work in the kitchen), so this is a room-label wobble, not a hard staging error. Awareness only.
- Voice: Bog is fully on card - the apology for the hair-pull ("Sorry. Sorry! Too rough... Grievance noted"), the "gen-gentle-man" sounded out like a foreign dish (his skewed Earth culture, exactly as his card describes), the surveillance offer made like checking the weather, and the shy directness of "It's for you too, Nia" - which is his most explicit statement of motive so far; his card says he usually presents devotion as mood and appetite, and the shyness marks this as the exception that proves it. Nia's deliberation is her card in action: compliance audited as reconnaissance ("It isn't surrender. It's reconnaissance. ...Isn't it?"), the spirits' criterion applied without being named (he listened, when nobody kept score), and the kiss-back framed as a field move ("a hand in the dark, feeling for the latch") - the ch007 smile-as-ruse pattern, advanced one step.
- "Your former mate" (Bog on Daniel): Bog's framing, not the narration's - Nia still calls Daniel her husband (ch005, ch007), and Bog elsewhere says "My husband would've fixed it" back to her without the "former". Character speech; no conflict.

## C. Craft, as a work

- This is the plot's ignition chapter: in 1,771 words it strikes the central deal, reveals the siphoning, Hermes, the fortress and the (first-draft) cartel plan, and lands the first kiss. Four turns, none of them rushed, because each reveal is carried by a scene with its own emotional job (the plea, the deliberation, the lap talk, the kiss).
- "Please" is the chapter's fulcrum and it is earned: seven chapters of counting, litanies and refusals make a single quiet word cost more than any speech. The staging helps - she says it with her scar whitening and her chin lifted, not begging downward.
- The acceptance device is the book in miniature: the meal that has been her humiliation and Bog's liturgy becomes a language - one long slurp for yes - and she fires it "a very long time", not looking up. It rhymes with ch007's closing retaliatory slurp (the same sound as weapon) and converts it into a signature on a contract. The strongest chapter ending since ch006.
- The lap-talk exposition dump (siphoning / Hermes / fortress / cartel in one conversation) is the riskiest move and it works, for two reasons: it is characterized (Bog tells state secrets between kisses, in fishing-plan voice - "Heh, heh"), and Nia's interior runs a counter-commentary that converts exposition into tension ("Every time he opens his mouth, he hands me another piece of the map").
- The kiss scene states the book's thesis from the other side: 412 men wanted the function, none wanted the person - "A kiss means you want the person. A kiss means you're looking at her." Her decision to kiss back as camouflage, and her fingers trembling for reasons she refuses to audit, is the deal's emotional price tag, paid in the same scene that signs it.
- Bog's moral tutoring begins here, quietly: "You'd get a whole cartel killed over a box of tools." / "That's bad, isn't it? Hm." / "It's bad." / "I'll think about it." - and the eventual raid indeed does not use a cartel massacre as its cover (see A2). Whether or not the later text intends the causality, the seed is planted in this exchange.
